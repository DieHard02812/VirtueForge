Still His Pick: Panel Sends McKee Three Names for Rhode Island’s First Inspector General — Then Asks Him to Wait Weeks

Virtue Forge briefing · Based on Rhode Island Current / News From The States reporting dated Sep 24, 2026

What happened

After interviewing five finalists Thursday at the State House, Rhode Island’s Inspector General Independent Advisory Commission selected David Harper, James Kirdar, and Stuart Robinson as the three candidates whose names go to Gov. Dan McKee for the state’s first inspector general. The five-member panel had through Sept. 28 to forward three names under the deadline set when the General Assembly created the office in the fiscal 2027 budget.

The shortlist is national, not State House-bred: Harper is inspector general for the U.S. Capitol Police; Kirdar is that office’s assistant inspector general for investigations (and a Roger Williams University criminal-science alumnus); Robinson directs investigations for Palm Beach County, Florida’s inspector general office after a 25-year FBI career that included public-corruption work in Boston and Bridgeport. A sixth finalist, U.S. Education Department acting IG Mark Priebe, withdrew Monday. The new office is authorized at 12 people; pay ranges $175,000–$225,000; the IG may serve five years with one possible reappointment of the same length.

The three names come with a built-in stall: commissioners say McKee should make no final selection until Rhode Island State Police background checks finish—a process Attorney General Peter Neronha, who sits on the panel, said could take six to eight weeks. McKee then has 90 days to nominate; the Senate must give advice and consent. Neronha also described the office’s statutory subpoena language as a “blank canvas,” unclear whether it reaches witness testimony or only records. The three finalists offered different approaches to subpoenas, investigation timelines, and how much of their findings they would publish.

Watchdog groups and lawmakers spent more than two decades pushing for an inspector general before House Speaker Christopher Blazejewski made the office a top priority in this year’s budget. The search panel is Neronha, General Treasurer James Diossa, Secretary of State Gregg Amore, Ethics Commission executive director Jason Gramitt, and Association of Inspectors General president Will Fletcher.

Fact check: Attribute the three names, the Priebe withdrawal, the 6–8 week background-check caveat, the 90-day gubernatorial clock, salary band, and “blank canvas” subpoena quote to Rhode Island Current’s Sep 24, 2026 report (Christopher Shea)—not to campaign spin. Do not claim McKee has already nominated anyone, that State Police checks are complete, or that the Senate has confirmed. Do not treat interview answers about subpoenas or transparency as enacted RI law; the statute’s scope is still contested. Resume claims (case counts, recommendation tallies) are the candidates’ reported credentials, not VF-verified audits. Creating the office is not the same as an IG already investigating fraud, waste, or abuse in Rhode Island agencies.

Virtue Forge commentary

Rhode Islanders spent twenty-plus years asking for an independent watchdog while the Assembly shrugged. Now the shortlist finally lands—and the pick still belongs to Dan McKee, with a weeks-long background stall and a Senate confirmation gauntlet after that. An inspector general is civic virtue only if it can actually shine light on fraud, waste, and mismanagement inside the same machine that resisted the office for a generation.

Bias check: This is not a hit piece on Harper, Kirdar, or Robinson’s résumés, and it is not a claim that out-of-state experience equals corruption. The civic point is institutional design: after decades of delay, the governor who lost his primary still chooses the first IG, the panel itself asks him to wait on State Police checks, and the AG on that panel says subpoena power is a blank canvas. Celebrate a real accountability structure; refuse to confuse a shortlist presser with sunlight already delivered.

Free people and free enterprise need auditors who can compel records, publish findings, and survive the political class they police—not another patronage slot dressed as reform. Rhode Islanders should watch whether McKee’s nominee gets real subpoena teeth, whether reports land in public rather than behind executive summaries, and whether the Senate treats advice-and-consent as loyalty theater. A watchdog deferred is still better than no watchdog—if Rhode Islanders keep the receipt book open until the first hard investigation ships.
