Four Months After Getting Its Schools Back From the State, Providence Is Losing Its Superintendent and Three Top Deputies, While Four Schools Run on Space Heaters

Virtue Forge briefing · Oct. 30, 2026 · Based on Boston Globe, WPRI 12, NBC 10 (WJAR), and Providence Journal reporting, plus a district School Building Committee presentation

What happened

Providence Public Schools Superintendent Javier Montañez has hired a private lawyer to negotiate an exit agreement with the Providence School Board, the Boston Globe reported this week, citing three people with direct knowledge of the talks. His contract runs through June 30, 2027, but the Globe reported that an announcement could come before the end of the year. On Friday, WPRI’s Target 12 posted a story headlined “Providence superintendent expected to announce exit.”

The district returned to local control in July, ending nearly seven years of state takeover. Since then, WPRI reported, three other senior officials have left: Deputy Superintendent of Operations Zack Scott resigned Sept. 25, Deputy Superintendent of Academics Paula Dillon resigned Oct. 16, and Chief of Staff Lisa DiMartino resigned Oct. 23. Before leaving, Scott told a chaotic, hourslong board meeting that he was “increasingly concerned” about his ability to do his “best work in this current environment.” At that meeting, board members largely argued over the performance of board President Ty’Relle Stephens and called for his removal.

State Rep. David Morales, the Democratic nominee for Providence mayor, confirmed to NBC 10 on Thursday that the city will be looking for a new superintendent and said his team hopes to help pick an acting one. “Things are moving relatively quickly, as we have seen,” he said. Stephens said in a statement that the board “has taken no official action.” NBC 10 reported that the board’s postponed vote on whether to remove Stephens as president is expected at its Nov. 18 meeting.

Meanwhile, the heat

The same week, the Providence Journal reported that boiler problems left students cold at four schools: Central High, Nathanael Greene Middle, Hope High, and George J. West Elementary. Hope High opened two hours late on account of the heating repairs, and the district shipped emergency space heaters to the other three. At Greene, so many space heaters were running that the whole second floor lost power, which matters because, as Providence Teachers Union President Lindsay Paiva noted, the curriculum runs on smart boards and computers. Teachers also reported cold rooms at Alvarez High, Leviton Dual Language School, and parts of Classical High.

The district said all its boilers passed state and contractor inspections this summer and that it is working with ABM, its facilities maintenance company, on repairs. As of Thursday, Greene’s boiler was running again; Central’s was repaired, but a state inspection turned up more problems; George West’s was still being fixed, and the district is looking at a new boiler there. At Hope High, the district said the boiler sat inside the work zone of a separate construction project “without PPSD’s knowledge or authorization,” so crews couldn’t reach it.

Fact check: (1) The exit isn’t official yet. As of this writing, Montañez hasn’t publicly announced a departure, the board says it has taken no official action, and the district says it doesn’t comment on personnel matters. Any severance figure hasn’t been reported. (2) Background confirmed by WPRI: Gov. Dan McKee and Education Commissioner Angélica Infante-Green named Montañez interim superintendent in June 2021 after Harrison Peters left amid a hiring scandal, then made him permanent in April 2022 amid criticism that the job was never posted. He signed a three-year, $200,000 contract in July 2024 with 3% annual raises. (3) The union’s charge: Paiva blames years of district mismanagement and misplaced financial priorities. That is an interested party’s view, but the district’s own statement confirms the four-school boiler outages. (4) Hope High’s boiler: A School Building Committee presentation dated Oct. 22 lists a $1,947,302 Hope High boiler project, with a temporary boiler planned for November and the permanent one finished by fall 2027. Reporting hasn’t said whether that is the project whose work zone blocked access.

Virtue Forge commentary

For seven years, Rhode Island ran Providence’s schools from the top. The state picked the superintendent, and as the Globe’s Dan McGowan, who broke the exit story, put it in a podcast interview this week, Montañez was “never quite the person actually in charge”; the commissioner was. When control came back to the city, it came back to a school board that has spent the fall fighting over its own president. Four months in, the four top people running the district are gone or on their way out, in the middle of a school year.

None of this is a case for sending the state back in. The state takeover installed a superintendent without posting the job and graded its own work. And today’s district can’t say how one of its own boilers ended up inside somebody else’s construction zone without its knowledge. Neither the state commissioner nor this school board has shown it can run a district well. Families deserve a stable superintendent picked through an open search, plus a plain public accounting of who is responsible for the buildings and the contractors working in them.

Bias check: Virtue Forge is skeptical of centralized state control and of the political class that runs Providence. To be fair, old boilers fail in plenty of districts on the first cold week, the district says its boilers passed inspection this summer, and repairs were moving. Leadership turnover after a takeover ends isn’t unusual, and the board is entitled to choose its own superintendent. Our criticism is about timing, transparency, and who answers for the results.

What to watch

• Whether Montañez announces his exit, and the terms of any severance agreement.
• Who the board names as acting superintendent, and how much say Morales gets in that choice if he wins Tuesday.
• The board’s Nov. 18 vote on removing President Stephens.
• Whether every school has working heat before the first hard freeze, and who approved the Hope High work zone.
