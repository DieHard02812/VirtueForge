Johnston Voters Said No to Broader Land-Taking Power. Town Hall Already Has a “Plan B”

Virtue Forge briefing · Based on the Johnston Sun Rise (Nov. 12, 2026), the Providence Journal (Nov. 12, 2026), Johnston’s unofficial election results, and federal court filings

What happened

On Nov. 3, Johnston voters rejected a proposed Town Charter amendment that would have strengthened the Town Council’s power to take private property by eminent domain. The town’s unofficial results page, last updated Nov. 10, shows the charter question (Question 6, Section 1-3, “Condemnation”) failing 4,761 (53.79%) to 4,090 (46.21%). The Johnston Sun Rise and the Providence Journal both report that it was rejected. These results are unofficial.

Town officials say they will keep going. The Sun Rise reports that the town will use its fallback, a Municipal Public Building Authority (MPBA), if it needs to acquire property for municipal use. An MPBA is a state-sanctioned board that can take private property for public buildings. The Journal’s headline: “Johnston voters rejected eminent domain plan. Town says fight isn’t over.”

How we got here

The fight is over 31 acres at 178 and 200 George Waterman Road. The land is owned by the Santoro family, doing business as SCLS Realty LLC and Sixty Three Johnston LLC, who planned a 252-unit affordable apartment complex there. The town moved to take the land for a municipal campus: a police and fire headquarters plus a Town Hall. The Santoros sued in U.S. District Court, represented by the Pacific Legal Foundation.

This summer, U.S. District Judge Melissa R. DuBose ruled for the family. The court entered summary judgment declaring the town’s attempt to seize the property “illegal, and void,” according to a later filing by the family. The Sun Rise reported that the court found the town “failed to guarantee just compensation to the landowners and failed to establish definite and specific procedures through which the property owners could legally enforce their right to payment.” A preliminary injunction against further condemnation steps, issued April 2, 2025, stayed in place, according to the family’s filing.

At a special meeting on July 31, the Town Council took up two resolutions. One put the charter amendment on the November ballot. The other created the MPBA as a backup. Councilman Robert Civetti told the Sun Rise that the council approved the MPBA 4-1, with him voting no. The ballot question read, according to the family’s federal court filing:

“Shall Section 1-3 of the Johnston Town Charter be amended to identify a fund, establish a pledge of credit, and establish an enforcement mechanism for the exercise of its condemnation authority?”

What they said

Town Council President Robert Russo, to the Sun Rise: “I don’t think a lot of people understood the question and a number of people didn’t even place a vote either way. The town still has the Municipal Public Building Authority, which was created in response to the court case involving George Waterman Road and I believe has eminent domain jurisdiction.”

Councilman Civetti: “Due to the lack of information available to the voters, many voters simply did not understand the question in full and as a result voted against the question as presented.” He also said: “In my opinion, if the town is unsuccessful in the existing eminent domain case, then that would be the direct result of actions taken by the administration and legal counsel during the eminent domain process and not the result of the voters rejecting the referendum question on November 3.”

Mayor Joseph Polisena Jr. declined to comment specifically because of the open lawsuit, the Sun Rise reports, but said he fully understands what voters wanted. Council members Lauren Garzone, Linda Folcarelli and Alfred Carnevale had no comment, citing the litigation.

Kady Valois of the Pacific Legal Foundation, in a Nov. 6 statement quoted by the Sun Rise: “Johnston voters sent a clear message: they do not want to give the town broader power to take private property. This is an important victory for property owners. But despite the public’s rejection of these eminent domain efforts, the town is still trying to take the Santoros’ land to prevent them from developing affordable housing on their own land.”

Fact check: “voters didn’t understand.” If the question was confusing, the town wrote it. As quoted in the court filing, the ballot language never uses the words “eminent domain” or “private property.” It refers only to a “fund,” a “pledge of credit” and “condemnation authority.” Even so, 8,851 votes were cast on it, and the “no” side won by 671. Russo says many voters skipped the question. We could not find a published undervote figure to check that, and skipped questions don’t change a result that turns on the votes actually cast.
Fact check: what did the court decide? After the ruling, the mayor posted on Facebook that “the court determined the Town does have the authority to take the land by eminent domain,” according to the family’s Aug. 4 filing, which includes a screenshot. The Sun Rise reported in the summer that Polisena noted the court did not say the town lacks eminent domain authority. Pacific Legal says the court found the town’s charter “lacked a general delegation of eminent domain power,” so the taking was void. Both sides agree the court threw out this taking and did not rule on every route the town might try next. The mayor’s post goes further than the ruling as both sides describe it.

Virtue Forge commentary

Here is the sequence. A federal judge voided the taking. Within days, the council came back with two ways to get the same power. Voters rejected the one they were allowed to vote on. Officials now say the voters misunderstood and point to the backup they approved without a public vote. That is the opposite of humility in government. When the people who would live with a power decline to grant it, the respectful response is to accept the answer, not to look for a different door.

Eminent domain is one of the most serious powers a local government has, and the Constitution guards it with requirements like just compensation and fair process. In this case, a court found Johnston fell short of those requirements. Its owners want to build 252 apartments on their own land in a state that never stops saying it needs more housing. Whatever one thinks of the project, a town that loses in court, then at the ballot box, and keeps going is showing how hard government power is to put back in the box once officials decide they want it.

Bias check: The Journal’s summary describes the charter change as a way “to create a coherent pathway to exercise eminent domain.” That is the town’s framing, and it leaves out what the pathway was for: taking one family’s land. In fairness to the town, a public safety complex is a legitimate public use, the owners’ lawyers have an interest in the outcome, and the case is still active. Officials say they can’t say much while it is pending.

What to watch: whether Johnston’s new MPBA moves to condemn the George Waterman Road parcels, how the federal court treats that given its injunction, and whether any council member asks to drop the taking now that voters have spoken.
