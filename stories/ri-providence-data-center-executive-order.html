Rhode Island

Providence Already Bans Data Centers Citywide. With Weeks Left in His Term, Mayor Smiley Signed an Executive Order Reaffirming the Ban and Asking the State to Set Rules for Every Other Town

Virtue Forge briefing · Based on the City of Providence press release (Nov 23, 2026) and NBC 10 News (Nov 23, 2026)

What happened

On Monday, Nov. 23, 2026, Providence Mayor Brett Smiley signed an executive order titled “Addressing the Environmental, Infrastructure, and Community Impacts of Data Center Development.” It came weeks before Mayor-elect David Morales takes over City Hall.

The order does not change what can be built in Providence today. In the city’s own words, “Under Providence’s existing Zoning Ordinance, data centers are not identified as an allowable principal use and are therefore currently prohibited citywide. The executive order reaffirms these existing restrictions while establishing a framework for developing comprehensive policies to address potential future proposals.”

The order tells the Department of Planning and Development, working with the Sustainability Department and other departments, to write a policy covering “whether and under what circumstances data centers should be permitted in Providence including any necessary limitations on the scale of development,” a definition of data centers, effects on electricity demand, water, infrastructure, noise, air quality and greenhouse gas emissions, zoning and permitting rules, and “any State action necessary to support policy implementation.” It acknowledges that the City Council controls zoning.

The order also goes beyond the city line. It “calls on the Governor, the Rhode Island General Assembly and all appropriate state agencies to consider statewide policies that address data center development” and asks for “a standard, statewide approach.” Smiley said: “Providence residents cannot shoulder the weight alone of higher utility costs because of large-scale developments elsewhere in Rhode Island… This issue is larger than our city and is one that requires statewide coordination. We are calling on our state partners to establish clear, consistent protections for all Rhode Islanders.”

Fact check: The order doesn’t name any Rhode Island data center that is raising anyone’s electric bill. The best-known proposal in the state, a business park with a data center in Smithfield, was banned from every zone by that town’s council in May and is now in court (our earlier story). Whether a big new electricity customer raises or lowers rates for everyone else depends on who pays for the power plants and transmission lines it needs. That is a utility-rate question the Public Utilities Commission can settle with cost rules. It doesn’t require a ban. The “data centers are prohibited” claim is accurate only in the sense the city describes: they aren’t a listed use, so they aren’t allowed.

Virtue Forge commentary

This is governing by press release. Data centers were already off-limits in Providence before Monday. The order keeps that ban, hands the question to the city’s planning and sustainability offices with emissions and “whether” data centers should be allowed at all on the checklist, and then asks the Governor and the General Assembly to write rules for Smithfield, Johnston, Burrillville and every other community too. A mayor who is leaving office is using his last weeks to set the terms of a debate his successor and other towns will have to live with.

Bias check: Concerns about water, noise and who pays for grid upgrades are legitimate, and a city can set reasonable rules about them. Our objection is to the approach: start from a ban, study whether to ever lift it, and push the state to impose one standard on 39 cities and towns. Rhode Island’s zoning is local for a reason. A community that wants the tax base should be able to say yes on its own terms.

Rhode Island keeps saying it wants a bigger commercial tax base so homeowners don’t carry the whole load. If utility costs are the worry, write a rule that makes large new users pay their own way on the grid and let projects compete under it. A city that keeps a blanket ban in place while it studies the question, and then asks the state to standardize that approach for everyone, is sending investors a simple message: don’t bother coming here.
