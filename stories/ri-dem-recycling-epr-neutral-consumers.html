“Neutral” for Consumers? Rhode Island’s Recycling Study Prices a Producer-Mandate Overhaul at Up to $77 Million a Year—and Assumes Shoppers Won’t Pay It

Virtue Forge briefing · Nov. 8, 2026 · Based on RIDEM’s draft Statewide Implementation Analysis and ecoRI News

What happened

The Rhode Island Department of Environmental Management (DEM) is taking public comment through Nov. 16, 2026, at 4 p.m. on a draft Statewide Implementation Analysis for a beverage container redemption (“bottle bill”), packaging, and recycling plan. The General Assembly ordered the study in 2025; DEM is running it with the quasi-public Rhode Island Resource Recovery Corporation (RIRRC) and an outside consultant. It compares five options: keeping today’s system (“As-Is”), a combined extended producer responsibility (EPR) and deposit program modeled on bills H6207 and S0996, EPR alone, a deposit system alone, and an “EPR Plus” plan that extends producer funding to businesses and institutions and adds reuse targets.

Under EPR, packaging and beverage companies would pay fees to a producer responsibility organization (PRO) that funds and runs recycling statewide. The draft’s own cost table, at full implementation in its medium scenario, puts the annual net cost of today’s system at about $13.2 million. The alternatives run about $22.0 million (deposit only), $49.5 million (EPR), $72.8 million (EPR plus deposit), and $77.0 million (EPR Plus). Net cost per ton collected rises from about $200 today to $370–$490 under the EPR plans.

At DEM’s Nov. 4 hearing, ecoRI News reported, longtime bottle-bill sponsor Rep. Carol McEntee (D-North Kingstown) argued neighboring deposit states are “not suffering.” Most other speakers were skeptical. Joshua Cloutier of North Kingstown-based Toray Plastics said his company would have to pass EPR costs on to customers. Bill Fischer of the Consumer Brands Association said the scenarios rely on assumptions, “including achieving a 65 percent recycling rate and reducing recycling facilities contamination from 21 to 11 percent,” that are “not supported by mature or verified performance data in U.S. packaging programs.”

Fact check: (1) The draft’s stakeholder table labels consumers “NEUTRAL” under the two EPR-only plans and charges them only for unredeemed deposits under the deposit plans. But the same section says producers “bear the largest cost because they are assigned whatever cost is not passed through to consumers or offset by commodity revenue,” and at least one Rhode Island manufacturer testified it would pass costs on. Treat “neutral” as a modeling label, not a promise about shelf prices. (2) The 65% recycling target and the drop in MRF contamination from 21% to 11% are the draft’s own figures (Table 2 and plan targets), as the industry witness said. (3) The draft also reports $1.9 billion in “Total Output” and 3,430 jobs under EPR Plus. Those are economic-activity estimates driven by new mandated spending, not net gains to Rhode Island households. (4) DEM’s notice calls the deadline “Thursday, November 16”; Nov. 16, 2026, falls on a Monday. The date and the 4 p.m. time are what DEM lists.

Virtue Forge commentary

Strip away the jargon and the “EPR” plans do something simple. They replace a roughly $13 million-a-year municipal recycling bill with a $50–$77 million-a-year system run by an industry-funded producer responsibility organization operating under a state mandate, plus new DEM oversight staff. The study then tells Rhode Islanders the cost is “neutral” for them because producers write the checks. Producers don’t print money. Fees charged per package end up in the price of the package, and a small state with thin margins for grocers and manufacturers is exactly where those costs show up first.

This is centralization dressed up as an environmental fix. Today Rhode Island’s 39 cities and towns choose their own haulers, contracts, and pay-as-you-throw experiments; the draft says collection and processing would “become standardized” statewide under EPR. The state’s own landfill operator is part of the problem the study is trying to solve. RIRRC’s recycling facility rejected 25,335 tons of recyclables in 2025, which were buried in Johnston, and its contamination rate sits at 21%. A Smith Hill fix that hands a bigger, costlier system to the same public apparatus, with targets an industry witness says no U.S. program has proven, deserves hard questions before it becomes a 2027 bill.

Bias check: Virtue Forge favors local control, price transparency, and free enterprise, and is skeptical of mandates that move costs off the public books without lowering them. Fairness: the draft is a study, not a law; deposit systems do work in neighboring states; the plans would cut municipal recycling costs and the draft projects longer landfill life and less litter. The Consumer Brands Association is an industry group with a stake in the outcome, and Beyond Plastics’ John Berard, who supports a bottle bill, also urged lawmakers to “pump the brake” on EPR.

What to watch

• Public comments to DEM through Nov. 16 at 4 p.m., and the final version of the analysis.
• Whether 2027 bills revive the H6207/S0996 combined EPR-deposit model the draft studied.
• Any shelf-price or pass-through estimates DEM adds in the final report, since the draft calls consumers “neutral.”
