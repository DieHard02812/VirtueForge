Big Government Can’t Even Keep Its Own Books: RI’s Largest Medicaid Plan Sues the State for $100 Million in Unpaid Bills

Virtue Forge briefing · Based on the Providence Journal (Nov. 10, 2026) and WPRI Target 12 (Eli Sherman, spring 2026)

What happened

Neighborhood Health Plan of Rhode Island, which WPRI calls the state’s largest Medicaid health plan, has sued the state’s Executive Office of Health and Human Services (EOHHS). The Providence Journal reports that the 20-page complaint was filed in Providence County Superior Court on Oct. 30, 2026. It alleges the state owes the nonprofit an estimated $100 million in unpaid Medicaid reimbursements after roughly a decade of underpayments.

Here is how the system is supposed to work. Rhode Island pays health plans like Neighborhood a monthly amount per Medicaid member, and the plans pay the doctors and nursing homes. The state alone decides each member’s eligibility and payment category. When a member’s care needs rise, the state is supposed to recategorize them and reconcile the payments. According to the complaint as reported by the Journal, the state for years failed to do that. It sometimes missed significant changes in patient care and sometimes did not enroll new members at all, while Neighborhood kept paying the bills.

The complaint, as quoted by the Journal: “The State has acknowledged its reconciliation problems since at least 2018, and when it reconciled a limited sample of members, it found large underpayments. Yet it has refused to reconcile the rest, most recently rejecting an audit that would cover all members and all years under the contracts.” It also says: “The State repeatedly promised Neighborhood that it would reconcile and pay the capitation owed for the members Neighborhood covered, including when the State’s own systems and processes had not caught up with those members’ care. The State still has not reconciled or paid what it owes.”

The suit brings four counts. It asks the court to order the state to complete the payments, subject to review by Neighborhood or a court-appointed independent auditor, and it seeks damages and legal fees. EOHHS declined to comment, citing the pending litigation, the Journal reports.

How we got here

This fight was public long before the lawsuit. WPRI’s Target 12 reported this spring that Neighborhood CEO Peter Marino wrote to Gov. Dan McKee in February warning of “legal remedies.” Marino said hundreds of members who got more than 90 consecutive days of skilled nursing care, or community-based services such as assisted living, were never put in the proper payment codes. He put the debt at $135.5 million through December 2023 ($114.3 million in one category and $21.2 million in another). The dispute dates to 2016, according to his letter. Neighborhood had already offered to settle for $100 million paid over four years.

At a Senate Finance hearing that week, then-EOHHS Secretary Richard Charest pushed back: “We don’t believe the state has a liability, but we’re willing to go through a process to evaluate.” He said the state was lining up an outside auditor. Senate Finance Chairman Louis DiPalma warned that if the underpayments are real, federal Medicaid matching money may not be available: “There’s a risk it’d all be general revenue.” Medicaid director Kristin Sousa agreed there was a risk and said CMS was being kept informed.

Fact check: The Journal’s headline says “unpaid Medicare costs,” but the dispute is about Medicaid, the state-run program, as the article’s own text says. The $100 million figure is Neighborhood’s estimate “under a proper reconciliation”; no court or auditor has validated it, and the state has said it doesn’t believe it owes the money. The figure is also lower than the $135.5 million Marino cited earlier, and it matches the discounted settlement Neighborhood offered. These are allegations in a lawsuit, not findings.

Virtue Forge commentary

Rhode Island’s health and human services bureaucracy runs the state’s Medicaid program and holds a monopoly on deciding who qualifies for what. By its own contracts, Neighborhood can’t set rates, determine eligibility, or categorize members; only the state can. So if the complaint is even half right, this isn’t a private insurer gaming the system. It’s a central agency that couldn’t keep its own records straight for years, sampled the problem, found underpayments, and then declined to look at the rest.

The bill for that kind of administrative failure doesn’t disappear. If a court or auditor agrees with Neighborhood, DiPalma’s warning means Rhode Island taxpayers could cover all of it from general revenue without the usual federal match. That makes it one more liability the state’s budget writers didn’t plan for. Before anyone proposes expanding what EOHHS manages, the state should show it can reconcile the accounts it already has. The audit Neighborhood says the state rejected is the cheapest way to find out who is right.

It’s worth noting who was in charge. Charest told lawmakers the state had no liability, then left in October to run CharterCARE, the state-backstopped hospital operator. That move is covered in our revolving-door story.

Bias check: Neighborhood is a plaintiff with a financial interest, and lawsuits put the most damaging version of events forward. The state hasn’t given its side yet beyond Charest’s earlier denial. Large Medicaid reconciliations are genuinely complex, and any settlement would involve federal regulators. What isn’t in dispute is that the disagreement has gone unresolved since at least 2016.

What to watch: the state’s formal answer to the complaint, whether an independent audit is ordered or agreed to, and whether any settlement shows up in the budget cycle.
