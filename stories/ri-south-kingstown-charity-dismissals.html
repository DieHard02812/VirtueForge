Pay a Charity, Skip the Courthouse: South Kingstown Police Ran a ‘Long-Standing’ Off-the-Books Way to Make Arrests Go Away, and Now the Town’s Failed Discipline Case Is Headed to Federal Court

Virtue Forge briefing · Based on the NBC 10 I-Team (Sept. 18, 2025; January 2026; Oct. 22, 2026) and the federal docket in Krajewski v. Town of South Kingstown Police Department, No. 1:26-cv-00785 (D.R.I., filed Oct. 22, 2026)

What happened

On Oct. 22, South Kingstown Police Lt. James Krajewski sued the police department, Chief Matthew Moynihan and the town in federal court in Providence, alleging malicious prosecution among other claims (NBC 10; docket 1:26-cv-00785). His lawyer, Lisa Holley, told NBC 10 she would file a similar suit Friday for Lt. Bethany Dolock.

The suit grows out of a practice that the lawsuit itself describes. According to NBC 10, it states: “The conduct at issue was a long-standing practice of the Department’s Prosecution Division, which Lt. Krajewski inherited, of resolving certain minor, first-offense cases through voluntary charitable donations, community service, or both. No police officer personally received money in exchange for dismissing a charge.”

How it worked, according to NBC 10’s sources

In September 2025, the I-Team reported that the department had suspended both lieutenants, its prosecution officers, with pay. Sources said the officers “allegedly contacted some people who had been arrested, with an offer for their case not to be resolved without going to court in return for a donation to a local charity.” According to NBC 10, “The arrangement meant those people who were arrested were not charged with a crime, didn’t go to court, and didn’t have their names entered into the court system where it would become a public record.” The arrests were for misdemeanor-level crimes, and the donations were “typically in the hundreds of dollars.” NBC 10 also reported: “There is no allegation that the officers enriched themselves.”

At the time, Moynihan wrote that “the officers’ conduct constituted egregious administrative violations of several department policies.” NBC 10 legal analyst Austin Dana asked: “Why are certain people given the opportunity to never go to the courthouse and never be there for an arraignment? And then another question is, where is the paperwork? Who is keeping tabs on who is making the contributions, whether the case should be dismissed or not dismissed?”

Then the case fell apart

In January 2026, NBC 10 reported that the department had dropped the allegations after disciplinary hearings began. The department’s attorney, Vincent Ragosta, wrote that the case “encountered unanticipated evidentiary and legal challenges.” He said the decision was not an exoneration, and both lieutenants were moved out of the prosecution unit.

A letter from the union’s lawyer, Carly Iafrate, said the chief’s decision “triggered a massive waste of time, money and resources” and that “there were no policies addressing the things that the Chief believed were problematic.” Moynihan said the department now has “a new policy that requires all criminal complaints to be presented to the District Court for adjudication and prohibits dismissals without court oversight.” The officers’ union voted “no confidence” in Moynihan in 2024.

What the lawsuit claims

According to NBC 10, the suit says Krajewski was told he was “under investigation for a crime” though there was never a criminal investigation. It says the AG’s Office and the U.S. Attorney declined to bring charges after Moynihan contacted them, and that “Moynihan acted intentionally, maliciously and with reckless disregard for Lt. Krajewski’s rights.” Krajewski is seeking damages and an order requiring the town to clear his name in a public statement.

Response: NBC 10 reports Moynihan said Thursday afternoon that he hadn’t seen the lawsuit and had no response, and he referred NBC 10 to the town solicitor. Moynihan is set to retire in early November.

Fact check: (1) What is established. That the practice existed is not just an anonymous-source claim. Krajewski’s own lawsuit calls it a “long-standing practice” of resolving minor, first-offense cases through “voluntary charitable donations, community service, or both.” The chief’s new policy, which bars “dismissals without court oversight,” confirms such dismissals were happening before. (2) What is not. How many cases, which charities and how much money are unknown. The “hundreds of dollars” figure comes from NBC 10’s sources. No officer is accused of pocketing money, no one has been criminally charged, and the disciplinary allegations were dropped. The suit’s claims against the chief are allegations. (3) Rank. NBC 10’s Oct. 22 story calls Dolock “Officer”; its earlier reports call her “Lt.”

Virtue Forge commentary

Two failures show up here, and taxpayers may pay for both. The first is the practice itself. For years, by the plaintiff’s own account, some people arrested in South Kingstown could make a charge disappear with a charity donation or community service. No judge was involved and no public court record was created. The police decided who got that deal, with no written policy, according to the union. Even if every donation went to a good cause and no officer gained a cent, that is not how equal justice is supposed to work. It means two tiers of treatment and no paperwork for the public to check.

The second failure is how the town handled it. The chief announced “egregious” violations, kept two lieutenants on paid suspension, and then dropped the case when it hit “unanticipated evidentiary and legal challenges.” Now the town faces two federal lawsuits. The real fix, court oversight of every dismissal, came only after the fact. Residents still have not been told how many cases were handled this way, or which charities got the money.

Bias check: Virtue Forge has no stake in who wins the lawsuit. Pre-court diversion can be good policy when it’s written down, applied evenly and overseen by a court. Our criticism is of a process with no rules, no records and no oversight. The town has not yet answered the suit.

What to watch

• Dolock’s lawsuit, expected Friday, and the town’s response in No. 1:26-cv-00785.
• Whether the town or Council discloses how many cases were resolved by donation, and to which charities.
• The costs to taxpayers of the paid suspensions, the dropped discipline case and the litigation.
• Moynihan’s retirement in early November and who replaces him.
