City Code Says Yes, State Ethics Rules Say Wait: Morales’s Team Waved Off the Revolving-Door Rule on a Sitting Councilor’s City Hall Job—Then Reversed After GoLocal Pressed

Virtue Forge briefing · Based on GoLocalProv (Nov 25, 2026), The Providence Journal (Nov 24, 2026), Providence Business News (Nov 24, 2026) and the Rhode Island Ethics Commission

What happened

On Tuesday, Nov. 24, 2026, Providence Mayor-elect David Morales named Ward 3 City Councilor Sue (Susan R.) AnderBois as his administration’s chief of policy, according to The Providence Journal and Providence Business News. The Journal describes her as the “outgoing Ward 3 city councilor” who will become “the chief policy-writer for the Morales administration.” AnderBois was also one of the co-chairs Morales named to his transition policy committees (our earlier story).

GoLocalProv asked whether hiring a sitting councilor could run into Rhode Island’s ethics rules on the revolving door. According to GoLocal, June Rose, executive director of the Morales transition team, said there was no violation. Rose cited the city’s own Code of Ethics ordinance, which says: “Nothing contained herein shall prohibit the mayor or the city council from appointing any elected official to a senior policy-making, discretionary, or confidential position on the mayor or the council’s staff, and in the case of the mayor, to a position as a department director or deputy director.” Rose added: “That provision very directly addresses this circumstance.”

GoLocal pointed out that a city ordinance does not override state ethics law. After more emails, Rose wrote: “Confirming that yes, we will have Sue request an advisory opinion from RIEC,” meaning the Rhode Island Ethics Commission.

The state rule is the Commission’s Regulation 1.5.4, “Municipal Official Revolving Door.” In the Commission’s words: “No municipal elected official or municipal school committee member, whether elected or appointed, while holding office and for a period of one year after leaving municipal office, shall seek or accept employment with any municipal agency in the municipality in which the official serves, other than employment which was held at the time of the official’s election or appointment.” The regulation lets the Commission grant an exception when doing so would not create an appearance of impropriety.

Accuracy check: Nobody has found that AnderBois broke any rule, and we don’t claim she did. The Commission can approve exceptions, and it has done so in past advisory opinions. Its ruling on this request is still pending. What’s documented is the sequence: the transition team first cited only the city ordinance and called an advisory opinion unnecessary, then agreed to seek one after GoLocal pointed to the state rule. Our sources don’t say when her council term ends or when the job starts.

Virtue Forge commentary

The revolving-door rule exists so that people holding public office don’t line up paid public jobs in the same government they oversee. That a sitting councilor who co-chaired the mayor-elect’s transition is now slated for his top policy job is exactly the kind of situation the rule was written for. You don’t need to assume bad faith to think the state ethics referee should look at it before the announcement, not after a reporter starts asking.

Bias check: Mayors are entitled to hire people they trust, and an experienced councilor may well be qualified to be chief of policy. Our objection isn’t to AnderBois personally. It’s to an incoming administration that answered an ethics question by citing its own city code, as if Providence could exempt itself from state ethics law, until someone outside City Hall pushed back.

Morales has promised to remake City Hall. If the new administration’s first instinct on a basic ethics question is “our ordinance says it’s fine,” voters should expect to keep watching closely. Doing it the right way is easy: file for the advisory opinion first, publish the answer, and then make the hire.
