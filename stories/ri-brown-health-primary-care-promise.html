Rhode Island

The Attorney General Suspended an Antitrust Investigation to Let Brown University Health Consolidate. The System Promised 40,000 New Primary Care Patients and at Least 15 More Clinicians. The First Report Shows a Net Loss of Three Providers and 260 New Patients

Virtue Forge briefing · Nov. 20, 2026 · Based on the Attorney General’s Sept. 26, 2025 agreement and Nov. 18, 2026 statement, Brown University Health’s July 1, 2026 performance report, and Rhode Island Current reporting

What happened

On Sept. 26, 2025, Attorney General Peter F. Neronha and John Fernandez, then president and CEO of Lifespan Corporation d/b/a Brown University Health, signed a seven-page letter agreement. In it, the Attorney General’s office agreed to suspend its antitrust investigation into the proposed combination of Brown Health Medical Group and Brown Physicians Inc. In exchange, the health system promised to expand primary care.

The binding patient target is specific. Brown Health Medical Group “will expand its primary care services to 40,000 net additional patients by December 31, 2029,” from a stated baseline of 191,700, for a total of at least 231,700. It also “shall hire no fewer than 15 net additional PCPs by December 31, 2027.” The system estimated that “at least twenty-seven (27) PCPs” would be needed for the added patients; that 27 figure is an estimate, not the minimum hiring commitment. The office reserved the right to reopen the investigation, with 30 days’ notice, and to take legal action if it believed the system was engaged in anticompetitive conduct or that the transaction had substantially lessened competition.

On Nov. 18, 2026, Neronha released the first performance report, which was due July 1 and covers the period from Oct. 1, 2025, through early June 2026. The report logged a net loss of three primary care clinicians as of June 1, 2026, and an increase of 260 net new patients against the 191,700 baseline. Neronha described that patient increase as “fewer than 300.” The Attorney General said 14 clinicians joined the system and 17 left during the period. The report itself said 17 primary care clinicians departed from Oct. 1, 2025, that five more were anticipated to leave before year-end, and that “these clinicians departing were responsible for nearly 15,000 patients.”

Neronha did not mince words. “Our review shows that while Brown University Health is actively recruiting primary care providers, the contemporaneous loss of existing primary care providers is holding back the progress this Office believes Brown University Health should be making by now,” he said. “Brown University Health’s inability to retain existing primary care providers is deeply concerning. While Rhode Island’s structural healthcare problems are well-documented and certainly play a role in recruitment and retention, Brown was aware of these challenges at the time of our agreement. That agreement, suspending this Office’s anti-trust investigation and allowing a consolidation of Brown University Health and Brown Physicians Inc., was premised on Brown increasing primary care capacity.” He added: “Accordingly, I am deeply concerned by Brown Health’s insufficient progress, and in my view a repeat performance in the next reporting session would warrant a significant response from this Office.”

The same report shows the system closed practices while it was supposed to be expanding access. It closed Narragansett Family Medicine in October 2025, after clinicians left for a concierge practice, and closed its Metacom practice in December 2025 after departures and a lease coming due. It also reported that only 73% of new patients overall were able to secure a visit within 14 days, 72% in adult and family medicine. The agreement requires “best efforts” to make a new-patient appointment available within 14 days; the reporting rules require an explanation whenever that share falls below 90%.

The baseline dispute, and what has happened since. The signed agreement represents the 191,700-patient baseline as the count “as of the Effective Date,” which was Sept. 26, 2025, the day both sides signed. In the July 1 report, Brown University Health said that figure “was calculated as of August 2025, not October 2025,” and that the actual Oct. 1, 2025, roster was 188,538. Using that later number, the system said, would show a net gain of 3,422 patients rather than 260. The Attorney General still described the official result as fewer than 300 net new patients. After the reporting period, Neronha said, four additional physicians had begun practicing by Sept. 30, 2026, and seven more clinicians were scheduled to start in fiscal 2027, and the system had cleared its primary care waitlist. Jessica Wharton, a Brown University Health spokesperson, told Rhode Island Current: “The recruitment of primary care providers is a top priority to ensure Rhode Islanders have timely access to care. We are backing our recruitment efforts with significant investments in competitive compensation, technology, clinical support, and resources that allow physicians to focus on caring for patients.” Sick-visit access in the report was strong: 96.8% of patients were seen within zero to one days.

Virtue Forge commentary

Rhode Island used its antitrust power not to finish a review of a hospital-system consolidation, or to block it, but to park the investigation in exchange for promises that come due in 2027 and 2029. The first official scorecard is now public. Against the baseline the system itself certified, it added 260 patients toward a 40,000-patient promise and ended the period with three fewer primary care clinicians, not more. It also closed offices in Narragansett and at Metacom. That is not a rounding error on a long ramp-up. It is movement in the wrong direction.

The Attorney General is right to call the retention failure “deeply concerning.” The question is what that concern is worth. The office can reopen the investigation with 30 days’ notice. It has not done so. Neronha says a “repeat performance in the next reporting session would warrant a significant response.” If the next report looks like this one, Rhode Islanders should be able to see what that response is, not another warning issued while the combination is already a fact.

Bias check: Virtue Forge is skeptical of deals that trade away a completed antitrust review for long-dated promises. To be fair, the hard deadlines have not arrived, Brown University Health is recruiting, it reports strong same-day sick-visit access, and the Attorney General says the waitlist has since been cleared. Primary care recruitment in Rhode Island is genuinely difficult, and some of the departures were retirements or clinicians leaving the state. Our objection is to treating a promise as a substitute for a finished review, then shrugging at a first report that goes backward.

What to watch

• The next performance report, due Jan. 15, 2027, and whether the net clinician count is still below the starting line.
• Whether the Attorney General reopens the investigation or takes other action, and what a “significant response” actually is if the next report is also thin.
• Whether the system meets the binding commitment of no fewer than 15 net additional primary care clinicians by Dec. 31, 2027, and the 40,000-patient target by Dec. 31, 2029.
