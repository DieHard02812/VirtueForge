Federal Watchdog Asked for the Price Check and Never Got It: $417,785 of Cumberland’s $1 Million Police Radio Grant Left Unsupported

Virtue Forge briefing · Nov 26, 2026 · Based on DOJ Office of the Inspector General Report 27-005 (posted Nov 18, 2026) and The Valley Breeze (Nov 25, 2026)

What happened

The U.S. Department of Justice’s Office of the Inspector General (OIG) has audited the $1,010,000 Community Oriented Policing Services (COPS) Technology and Equipment Program grant awarded in 2024 to the Town of Cumberland. The money paid for new hand-held and in-vehicle police radios and for upgrades to the Cumberland Police Department’s dispatch consoles and radio network. The report, No. 27-005, was posted Nov. 18 and lists $417,785 in questioned costs and six recommendations.

The headline finding is about price verification. Cumberland bought the equipment off a Rhode Island statewide procurement contract, and the OIG notes that it still had to check vendor pricing against the contract rates. The town did that for the radios ($592,215). It did not do it for the dispatch consoles and radio network. Those were the $417,785, or 41 percent of grant costs. When auditors asked for documentation so they could do a price comparison after the fact, the report says, “As of August 2026, Cumberland did not provide us with the requested documentation. As a result, we were unable to verify that $417,785 in grant funds were used to purchase equipment at a reasonable price.” The report also notes that the Mayor’s Office price-check routine “was not part of Cumberland’s documented policies or procedures.”

The project also cost more than the grant. According to the OIG, by the time the award came through, the plan had changed to include radios and equipment with improved technology. That raised the total project cost about 14 percent, to $1,150,545. “To address the $140,545 cost increase, Cumberland authorized a 3-year local funding payment plan, which included $16,202 in interest.” The dispatch and network upgrade was delayed an estimated 8 months. It was not installed and operational until June 2026, against an initial completion date of October 2025.

Federal regulators weren’t kept informed. Of three required semi-annual performance reports, one was filed 9 days late and one 29 days late. The third was filed 152 days early, “too early to reflect the entire project period.” None of them included all the information needed to tell COPS about the cost increases and delays. The auditors reviewed four quarterly Federal Financial Reports and found that “all the FFRs we reviewed contained financial inaccuracies.” Only one of the four was filed on time. Because the reports were late, COPS blocked drawdowns of grant money for most of the grant period. The OIG also found that Cumberland drew down $62,667 in excess funds in June 2026 and was still holding it in August, past the 10-day limit. And the $417,785 in dispatch and network equipment was never recorded in a property management system. Town officials told auditors “they were unaware of the property management requirements.”

The explanation from the department: “The Cumberland PD grant manager told us that the issues we identified in these reports resulted from prioritizing essential police department functions over grant reporting requirements.” The OIG found that Cumberland lacked written policies for performance reports, financial reports, price verification and drawdowns.

The program itself delivered. The OIG writes: “We confirmed that Cumberland purchased and deployed hand-held and in-vehicle radios. We also confirmed that Cumberland’s radio network and dispatch consoles were upgraded. We determined that these steps improved communication within Cumberland and among neighboring law enforcement agencies.” The auditors found Cumberland made “adequate progress” toward the grant’s goal, found no commingled funds, and found no grant-funded equipment lost, stolen or damaged.

Accuracy check: Questioned costs are not a finding of fraud, and nothing in the report alleges theft or wrongdoing. “Unsupported” means Cumberland lacked the documentation to show the $417,785 matched the state contract price. The OIG notes that questioned costs can be remedied in several ways, including by “the provision of supporting documentation.” The report found 100 percent of the costs were allowable and properly allocated, and it calls the state contract an encouraged way to buy. The COPS Office concurred with all six recommendations. The OIG says “Cumberland stated its agreement without providing a formal written response.” The report does not say whether the equipment was actually overpriced. We found no public statement from the town beyond what the report records.

Virtue Forge commentary

Federal grant money is still taxpayer money, and much of it comes from Rhode Islanders’ own paychecks. It doesn’t become free just because it arrives with a federal seal. The deal that comes with a grant is simple: you get the money, and you show your work. Checking that a vendor actually charged the state contract price isn’t red tape stacked on top of the job. For anyone spending other people’s money, it is the job. A private business that bought $417,785 of equipment and couldn’t show its auditor the price check would have a very uncomfortable meeting with its owners.

Then there’s the overrun. The project grew after the grant amount was set, and local taxpayers are now covering $140,545 on an installment plan that adds $16,202 in interest. That interest buys no radios and no officers. It’s the price of committing to an upgrade the budget hadn’t caught up with. Meanwhile the late, incomplete reports meant the agency paying the bill didn’t hear about the overrun or the delays in time to help.

Bias check: Cumberland got what the grant was for. Officers have working digital radios, and dispatch can now talk to neighboring departments, which matters a great deal in an emergency. The OIG confirmed that, and found nothing lost or misused. A department that puts policing ahead of federal paperwork is making an understandable choice. And the state contract the town used is supposed to guarantee a fair price in the first place.

Still, “we were busy policing” can’t be the standing excuse for skipping the bookkeeping that keeps public money honest. The fix is cheap: write down the price-check routine the Mayor’s Office says it already follows, put the dispatch gear on the property books, file reports on time, and hand the OIG the documents it asked for. If Cumberland’s purchase was fairly priced, the paperwork will prove it. Good stewardship is mostly showing the receipts.
