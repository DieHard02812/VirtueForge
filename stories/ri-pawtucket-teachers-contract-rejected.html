Rhode Island

Pawtucket’s Council Won’t Sign a Check Nobody Showed It Can Cash: $17 Million Teachers Contract Rejected 5–3 as the State Warns of a $10–15 Million Aid Cut

Virtue Forge briefing · Nov 25, 2026 · Based on NBC 10 News (Nov 24, 2026) and WPRI 12 News

What happened

On Tuesday night, Nov. 24, 2026, the Pawtucket City Council voted 5–3 to reject a $17 million, three-year teachers contract, NBC 10 reported, “after hundreds of teachers rallied outside City Hall ahead of the packed meeting.” The Pawtucket Teachers Alliance and the School Committee reached the agreement in August and sent it to the council for final approval. WPRI reports that the contract “has been in limbo since it was ratified back in August.”

According to WPRI, the contract would have raised salaries 3% in each of the first two years and 2% in the third. It also would have added 20 minutes to the elementary school day and reduced overall class sizes.

City Council President Terrence Mercer said the city could not afford the deal. “There were numerous questions about affordability,” Mercer told NBC 10. “Questions that the school committee clearly never asked.” Finance Committee Chairman Mark Wildenhain said: “It doesn’t mean you don’t deserve what you negotiated. It means we need to give the taxpayers what they deserve.”

The money worry is not hypothetical. Council members and Superintendent Randy Buck said the Rhode Island Department of Education projects “a $10 million to $15 million reduction in state aid in the coming years,” NBC 10 reported. WPRI says the state “just warned the district that it may lose anywhere between $10 million to $15 million in state support due to changes in funding.”

Pawtucket Teachers Alliance President Ronald Beaupre said: “They have had this contract on their desk for 91 days. We negotiated in good faith. We came to an agreement. We expect it to be approved.” He told WPRI that teachers “are abiding under the terms of the new agreement while the school department is abiding by the terms of the expired contract, meaning we’re working additional hours and not getting paid for it.” Outgoing Mayor Don Grebien urged councilors to approve the contract during public comment. Mayor-elect Adam Greenman remains hesitant, according to WPRI. He has asked for a full briefing on the agreement and its financial implications, and he said: “I’m hopeful that the current city leadership and stakeholders can work toward a resolution that’s fair to our educators, students, and taxpayers.”

WPRI reports this is the first time the Pawtucket City Council has rejected an already-ratified teachers’ union contract. Beaupre said the union would begin considering legal options.

Accuracy check: Our sources don’t say how each councilor voted, and they don’t give a year-by-year cost breakdown. The $10–15 million figure is a state projection of possible aid losses, not a final budget number. Whether the union has any legal path to force the contract through is still unresolved.

Virtue Forge commentary

The council did something governments rarely do: it said no to a spending commitment it couldn’t see how to pay for. A three-year contract binds future budgets, and the state has just warned that those future budgets may be $10 million to $15 million lighter. Signing anyway would have meant promising raises now and leaving the bill to taxpayers, or to cuts in classrooms, later. In Mercer’s words, the affordability questions were ones “the school committee clearly never asked.” That’s the real failure here. A multi-year deal was negotiated and ratified while those affordability questions went unanswered, and the council was left to be the adult in the room.

Bias check: Teachers have a real grievance. They say they are already working the longer hours without the new pay, and the contract had real benefits for students: more instructional time in elementary schools and smaller classes. Projections are projections, and state aid could come in better than feared. Rejecting the deal doesn’t mean teachers don’t deserve raises, and Wildenhain said as much. It means the raises have to come with a plan to pay for them.

The fix isn’t complicated. The School Committee and the union should come back with a deal the city can show it can fund in all three years, with the numbers in public, before the mayor-elect takes office and before lawyers take over. Fiscal restraint protects teachers too. A contract the city can’t pay for doesn’t help anyone.
