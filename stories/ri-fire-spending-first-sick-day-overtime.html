Rhode Island Spends More Per Person on Fire Protection Than Any State. Fires Are 2% of the Calls, and State Law Lets Sick Days Count Toward Overtime

Virtue Forge briefing · Nov. 16, 2026 · Based on a Rhode Island Current commentary (Nov. 16, 2026), the Rhode Island Public Expenditure Council report on police and fire spending (Aug. 31, 2026), and R.I. Gen. Laws § 28-12-4.1

What happened

On Monday, Nov. 16, Rhode Island Current ran a commentary by Joe Broadmeadow, who writes that he spent 20 years with the East Providence Police Department. Its title: “Rhode Island pays the highest prices for public safety. Where is the receipt?” His argument rests on an August report from the Rhode Island Public Expenditure Council (RIPEC). His question is one taxpayers rarely get answered: what do we get for the money?

What RIPEC found, according to its executive summary:

• In fiscal 2024, Rhode Island cities and towns spent $495.5 million on police and $436.4 million on municipal fire departments, about $931 million combined. Independent fire districts spent another $48.3 million.
• Rhode Island’s fire protection spending ranks first among U.S. states per capita and relative to personal income: 1.8 times the U.S. average per person.
• Police spending ranks ninth per capita and sixth relative to income.
• About 70% of fire department calls in 2023 were for emergency medical services. Fire-related calls were about 2%.
• Rhode Island had the fourth-lowest violent crime rate and the third-lowest property crime rate among states in 2024.
• Rhode Island ranked first in professional firefighters per capita in 2025, nearly twice the national rate. Its median firefighter salary ranked 12th.
• Pensions ate up 28.6% of municipal fire spending and 22.9% of police spending. In ten communities pension costs topped $50,000 per active member, and eight of those ten had a pension plan in “critical status,” meaning under 60% funded.

“Rhode Island spends considerably more on police and fire protection than most of the country, particularly on fire protection,” RIPEC President and CEO Michael DiBiase said when the report came out, as quoted by Ocean State Media. “Yet there is little evidence that the state has a greater need for these services.”

Broadmeadow singles out one cost driver set in state law rather than by local voters: a 2019 overtime mandate. Under R.I. Gen. Laws § 28-12-4.1(c), no city, town, or fire district may work a firefighter more than a 42-hour average week, figured over the prior eight weeks, without paying time-and-a-half. The statute adds: “For the purposes of this section, ‘hours worked’ shall include all paid leave.” As The Associated Press reported in 2019, then-Gov. Gina Raimondo let the bill become law without her signature, “despite strong opposition from municipal leaders.”

His proposed fixes: require every city and town to publish an annual public safety report in a standard format; test dedicated medical units instead of sending a full engine company to every medical call; and have the General Assembly attach a fiscal note to every public safety staffing or overtime bill, “starting with a fresh look at the paid-leave provision in the 42-hour law.”

Fact check: the commentary’s claims hold up, with one framing caveat. We checked Broadmeadow’s numbers against RIPEC’s report and the statute, and they match. The headline’s “highest prices for public safety” is true for fire protection, which ranks first. Police ranks ninth, so public safety as a whole is high but not first. On overtime: a 2021 Hummel Report investigation in the Providence Journal estimated that counting paid leave as hours worked was costing Warwick taxpayers close to $500,000 a year. In 2021 testimony, the Rhode Island League of Cities and Towns cited that same figure and noted that the Warwick practice “appears to predate the 2019 law.” So treat $500,000 as a sign of how expensive the rule can be, not a measure of the 2019 law alone. The League also told lawmakers the 42-hour mandate “appears to be the only one of its kind in the nation.” RIPEC itself notes there is no data separating what fire departments spend on medical calls from what they spend on fire suppression.

Virtue Forge commentary

Rhode Island pays first-place prices for fire protection in a state with one of the lowest fire death rates in the country. Seven of every ten fire calls are medical. Nobody can tell taxpayers what the money actually buys, because the system doesn’t measure it. That isn’t a knock on the firefighters and officers who show up. It’s a failure of the people who are supposed to manage the spending.

The 2019 overtime law shows how Rhode Island gets there. Federal labor law already sets overtime rules for firefighters. As the League of Cities and Towns told lawmakers, federal rules don’t require overtime until 212 hours in 28 days. The General Assembly overrode that for every city, town, and fire district, then defined a sick or vacation day as “hours worked.” The League said it knew of “no other job – in the public or private sector” where that is allowed. And according to the League, state employees are barred from doing the same thing for exactly that reason. The state passed the costs down to cities and towns, and property taxpayers pay them. When a 2021 bill to roll back the paid-leave provision came before the House Labor Committee, the Hummel Report says the committee held it for further study.

Bias check: Credit to Rhode Island Current for running this. It’s a rare case of a former officer asking for receipts on public safety budgets. The commentary goes out of its way to say nothing in it “cuts a single firefighter or police officer,” and most of its fixes are about transparency. That’s the right place to start. But the 42-hour paid-leave rule is a state law the legislature could repeal tomorrow, and it deserves more than “a fresh look.”

Gov.-elect Foulkes says she wants discipline in her first budget, and this is a good test. Local control means the people paying the bill decide how it’s run, not a state mandate written at the State House and billed to cities and towns. Start by repealing the paid-leave provision. Then publish the call data, staffing and overtime numbers for every department, and let voters judge what they’re paying for.

What to watch: whether any 2027 bill takes on the paid-leave provision in § 28-12-4.1, and whether Foulkes’s budget offers municipal mandate relief.
