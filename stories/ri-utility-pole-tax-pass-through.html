Taxation Without Representation, on Your Electric Bill: Johnston Taxes RI Energy’s Poles at 11 Times Charlestown’s Rate — and Ratepayers Statewide Cover It

Virtue Forge briefing · Nov 27, 2026 · Based on Arlene Violet’s GoLocalProv column (Nov 26, 2026), Ken Block’s Substack (Nov 17, 2026), RI Public Utilities Commission Docket 25-45-GE (Schedule SAB-7-ELEC (C)), the RI Division of Municipal Finance FY2026 tax-rate table, and R.I. Gen. Laws § 44-5.3-3

What happened

In a GoLocalProv column published Nov. 26, former Attorney General Arlene Violet called attention to a cost buried in every Rhode Island Energy bill. Each city and town taxes the utility’s poles, wires, transformers and substations as tangible personal property, at whatever rate it sets. RI Energy pays those bills, and the total goes into the rates every customer pays. Violet was building on a Nov. 17 analysis by former gubernatorial candidate Ken Block. Her column says: “You have the Rhode Island General Assembly and local towns and cities to thank for your shock since they have set up a statutory scheme which makes you pay for other people’s property taxes who live there.”

We checked the core numbers against primary sources, and they hold up. The state Division of Municipal Finance’s FY2026 rate table (assessment date Dec. 31, 2024) lists personal-property rates for all 39 municipalities. They run from $5.74 per $1,000 in Charlestown to $64.65 per $1,000 in Johnston, a ratio of about 11.3 to 1. The median is $20.65, the figure Block used. Johnston is far from alone at the top. Smithfield ($59.74), North Providence ($58.58), East Providence ($56.81), Providence ($53.40) and Pawtucket ($52.09) are all above $50.

The pass-through is documented at the Public Utilities Commission. In RI Energy’s base-rate case, Docket 25-45-GE, the company’s August 2026 compliance schedule SAB-7-ELEC (C) shows $54,862,208 in electric-side municipal taxes on its books for the test year ended Aug. 31, 2025. Of that, $47.5 million is tax on personal property. The company removed $17.3 million as transmission-related and allocated 68.39% of the rest to distribution. The result is $40,816,333 in municipal taxes built into electric distribution rates for the rate year ending July 31, 2027. The gas-side schedule, SAB-7-GAS (C), adds another $43,234,493 in municipal taxes for the same rate year. The schedule also shows these taxes growing: the company’s three-year average increase in distribution property taxes is 6.5% a year.

The same schedule shows how much individual towns collect. On electric personal property alone, RI Energy’s pro forma 2025 bill is about $16.0 million to Providence (a $308.5 million assessment at 51.99), $3.1 million to Johnston (a $48.7 million assessment at 64.58) and $3.6 million to North Smithfield (an $81.7 million assessment at 43.61). Charlestown collects about $70,000 (a $12.3 million assessment at 5.72).

State law made the spread permanent rather than closing it. As part of the 2023 budget, which created a statewide $50,000 tangible-tax exemption, the General Assembly enacted R.I. Gen. Laws § 44-5.3-3. It provides that “the tax rate for the class of property that includes tangible personal property for any city, town, or fire district shall be capped and shall not exceed thereafter the tax rate in effect for the assessment date of December 31, 2022.” The companion section, § 44-5.3-4, says tangible rates “shall be disregarded” when the state checks the usual limits on how far one property class’s rate may exceed another’s. Johnston’s own May 2026 tax notice says its $64.65 tangible rate “has been capped” under that section. The cap stops rates from climbing further. It also locks in whatever rate each town had at the end of 2022, high or low.

Block’s fix: “The legislature should remove any municipality’s ability to set a tangible tax rate for all RI utilities. Each town should use the same state-set rate.” He would also have the state value utility assets and phase the change in over several years. He estimates that a uniform $20.65 rate would charge electric ratepayers “about $21.6 million less” a year and that Providence “would lose close to $10 million.” Our own rough calculation from the PUC personal-property schedule lands in the same range: about $20 million statewide and about $9.7 million for Providence.

Accuracy check: The column gets several details wrong. Johnston’s rate is $64.65, not $64.75 (the state table and Block both say $64.65), and the low-rate town is Charlestown, not “Charleston.” The column says North Smithfield “receives over 13% of total property taxes collected from RI Energy.” Block’s actual claim is different: RI Energy pays North Smithfield almost $5 million in gas and electric taxes, which he puts at over 13% of the property tax that town collects. On the PUC schedule, North Smithfield’s electric share comes to about $4.4 million, roughly 8% of RI Energy’s statewide electric municipal tax bill. We did not independently verify the 13%-of-levy figure. The “$50 million per year” is total electric-side municipal tax. About $40.8 million of it lands in distribution rates, and the transmission-related share is handled outside distribution rates. Finally, not all of that money is a “subsidy.” Only the part above a common rate is shifted from one town’s customers to everyone else’s. The column’s separate claims about state energy mandates are outside this briefing.

Virtue Forge commentary

This is taxation without representation, in miniature. A family in Charlestown, Westerly or Little Compton has no vote in Johnston, Smithfield or Providence. Yet every month, through the distribution charge, it pays part of the property-tax bill those councils send the electric company. A council that taxes poles and wires at $50 or $60 per $1,000 doesn’t send most of that bill to its own voters. It sends it to ratepayers across the state, who have no way to vote it down. That is a recipe for overtaxing, and the numbers look like it.

It is also a hidden tax. No line on your bill says “Johnston property tax.” It is folded into a regulated rate and labeled delivery. Honest government should be visible government. If a city wants revenue, its own taxpayers should see the bill and judge it at the ballot box. When the cost is spread silently across 39 communities’ electric bills, accountability disappears. And the PUC filing shows this cost rising about 6.5% a year.

Bias check: Host communities aren’t simply freeloading. Substations and lines occupy land, and towns provide roads, police and fire protection around them. Tangible rates also apply to every local business, not just the utility. Several of the high-rate places are older cities with heavy budgets, and a uniform rate would leave holes in them that would likely fall on their own homeowners. Block himself proposes a phase-in and possible state help. And the 2023 cap did stop tangible rates from rising, which helped businesses.

Still, the cap protected the status quo, not the ratepayer. The fair, free-market answer is a single rule that applies to everyone: one statewide rate for utility property, set by the legislature that answers to all Rhode Islanders, with state valuation and a predictable phase-in. Cities that want to spend more can ask their own voters. If the General Assembly wants lower power bills, this is a place to start that costs no new program, no new agency and no new subsidy. Just stop letting a few town halls tax the whole state through the meter.
