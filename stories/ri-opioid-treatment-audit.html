Rhode Island

Every Claim Failed: Auditor General Finds BHDDH’s Opioid-Clinic Oversight Didn’t Work—and Medicaid Paid $875,696 in Daily Methadone Bills It Should Have Bundled

Virtue Forge briefing · Based on the RI Office of the Auditor General’s performance audit “Behavioral Healthcare, Developmental Disabilities & Hospitals – Provider Compliance with Opioid Treatment Program Requirements” (report date Oct. 7, 2026, conducted with the HHS Office of Inspector General), including the BHDDH and EOHHS responses printed in it, plus reporting by WPRI 12 and Uprise RI (Oct. 8, 2026)

What happened

On Wednesday, Oct. 7, Rhode Island’s Office of the Auditor General, led by Auditor General David A. Bergantino, released a performance audit of how the state Department of Behavioral Healthcare, Developmental Disabilities and Hospitals (BHDDH) oversees the state’s opioid treatment programs. These are the licensed methadone clinics that bill Medicaid. The audit was done jointly with the federal HHS Office of Inspector General. It covers Jan. 1, 2023 through Dec. 31, 2024, when five providers billed Medicaid for 274,412 medication-assisted treatment services totaling $25,541,470.

The headline finding is blunt. Auditors pulled a random sample of 100 of those services, and every one of the 100 “failed to meet at least one compliance requirement.” Across 88 federal and state requirements, they counted 1,729 deficiencies. They found 99 instances of noncompliance with medication-assisted treatment requirements, 95 involving the biopsychosocial assessments required at intake or at the five-year mark, 75 involving six-month treatment-plan reviews, 70 involving required counseling, 51 involving annual assessments, 50 involving annual treatment plans, 42 involving annual medical exams and 22 involving drug tests. The report calls this “a systemic lack of compliance.”

The audit puts the cause on the regulator. BHDDH’s licensing unit did audit all five providers every two years. But the report says those biennial audits “were not effectively ensuring provider compliance,” and that BHDDH “did not compile and evaluate provider results in a manner sufficient to determine if its monitoring procedures were effectively ensuring compliance.” Projecting from the sample, auditors estimated that services worth $24,921,184 did not meet federal and state requirements. The report calls that “the lower amount in the range of potentially unallowable costs” estimated by the federal inspector general.

The second finding is about money that plainly shouldn’t have gone out. Rhode Island’s Medicaid State Plan requires methadone treatment to be billed as one bundled claim per patient per week, and the audit says daily billing “is not authorized.” Auditors nonetheless identified and validated 6,347 claims in which Medicaid paid providers for daily methadone visits instead. In many cases, providers sent in five to seven daily claims for the same patient in one week, “causing the State to pay several times more than the established weekly bundled rate.” The result was $875,696 in potential overpayments, of which $656,919 is the federal share. The auditors blamed provider noncompliance and “the absence of adequate system controls within Medicaid systems,” meaning no edits in the managed-care or state encounter-data systems to catch the billing. In the report’s words, “RI Medicaid did not ensure that controls were in place to prevent or detect the improper payments.”

Both agencies’ responses are printed in the report. BHDDH promised unannounced visits, six-month follow-up reviews, provider self-audits at least every six months, standardized templates, mandatory training, a compliance threshold of about 90%, and a quarterly compliance dashboard. It also said it “will maintain its established biennial audit protocol.” The Executive Office of Health and Human Services (EOHHS), which runs Medicaid, said corrective-action plans “that include recoupments are in the process of being reviewed.” It said it is working with its Medicaid-systems contractor, Gainwell Technologies, on guardrails. It also said it is “in the process of confirming with Gainwell that a system correction was implemented in 2024.” Neither agency gave completion dates.

Fact check: The “every claim failed” finding is about a random statistical sample of 100 services (90% confidence level), not a review of every claim. A sampled service counted as a failure if it missed any one requirement. Many of the failures are documentation gaps (missing or late assessments, plans or exams), not proof that a patient went without treatment. The $24.9 million figure is a statistical projection of costs that may be unallowable for Medicaid. It is not a finding of fraud or a repayment order. The $875,696 covers specific claims the auditors validated, but the report still calls them “potential” overpayments, and the money has not been recovered yet. The audit does not name the five providers or allege fraud. EOHHS said only that its monitoring “may” lead to further recoupments and possibly a fraud referral to the Medicaid Fraud Control Unit. One oddity: EOHHS’s response to the methadone-billing finding mentions updates to “the transportation provider’s corrective action plans.” That appears to be language carried over from an unrelated document. The audit did not review the state’s opioid-settlement funds, which are tracked separately.

Virtue Forge commentary

Rhode Island taxpayers fund a regulator for these clinics, and federal and state dollars together pay the clinic bills. This audit says both safeguards failed at once. The regulator went through its routine every two years but never checked whether the routine was working. And the Medicaid payment system, run under contract by a major vendor, had no basic edit to stop it from paying five, six or seven claims in a week where the state’s own plan allows one. The second failure is not a judgment call about clinical paperwork. It is a rule written into the state’s own Medicaid plan, and the state’s systems did not enforce it.

This is the recurring problem with centralized programs: the agency is graded on whether it held its meetings and filed its reviews, not on whether the money bought what it was supposed to. It took outside auditors, state and federal, to find what the agency’s own process missed. Now the agency’s fix is mostly more process: dashboards, templates, office hours. That is better than nothing. But the measure that matters is whether Rhode Island actually claws back the overpayments, returns the federal share, and shows the public that the payment edit is in place and tested. Right now EOHHS cannot even say for sure whether a 2024 fix went in.

Bias check: Methadone clinics treat a very sick population, and Rhode Island’s overdose deaths have fallen from their 2022 peak of 436, according to the report’s own background section. Documentation rules are detailed (88 requirements), and some gaps reflect strained clinic staffing more than bad faith. The audit found 95% compliance on progress notes and on medical services and care coordination. Nobody is accused of a crime, and both agencies accepted the recommendations. The criticism here is about oversight and payment controls, not about the patients or the treatment model.

What to watch: whether EOHHS announces a dollar amount recovered from the five providers and returns the $656,919 federal share; whether it confirms the Gainwell system edit is live and tested; whether any matter is referred to the Medicaid Fraud Control Unit; and whether BHDDH posts the promised compliance dashboard and sets dates for any of its commitments. The report lists none.
