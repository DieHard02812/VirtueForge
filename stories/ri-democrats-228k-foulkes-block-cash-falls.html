RI Democrats Put $228K in Mailers and Staff Behind Foulkes in Three Weeks as Block’s Cash Falls 76%

Virtue Forge briefing · Oct. 28, 2026 · Based on the candidates’ own R.I. Board of Elections “2026 7 Days Before Election” CF-2 reports (period Oct. 6–Oct. 26, 2026)

What the filings show

The final pre-election campaign-finance reports in the governor’s race are posted on the Board of Elections’ public ERTS site. They cover the three weeks from Oct. 6 through Oct. 26, the last full stretch before the Nov. 3 election.

Democrat Helena Buonanno Foulkes (report) started the period with $214,871.74, took in $421,980.19 from individuals and $29,767.21 from PACs, returned $5,219.27 in contributions, and spent $541,219.61. She ended with $120,180.26 in cash, down about 44%. Most of the spending, $498,636, went to Canal Partners in four advertising payments.

Her report also lists $237,168.84 in in-kind contributions. Of that, $228,124.99 came from the Rhode Island Democratic State Committee in three entries:

• $153,459.77 for “Production & Postage Mailers” (Oct. 13)
• $66,713.15 for “Production & Postage Mailers” (Oct. 26)
• $7,952.07 for “Employee Salaries and Wages” (Oct. 15)

That is about $220,000 in mailers and about $8,000 in staff pay in three weeks. It is roughly half as much as Foulkes raised in cash from individuals and PACs over the same period. The rest of her in-kind support, about $9,000, came from individuals and three PACs (event food, catering, entertainment and digital ads). In her previous report (Sept. 2–Oct. 5), the party gave $126,596.34 in in-kind support, mostly digital communications and salaries, as we reported then. That brings the party’s reported in-kind support since Sept. 2 to about $354,700. Foulkes’ report still carries $1,835,000 in loans payable, which press reports describe as money she lent her own 2022 campaign.

Independent Ken Block (report) started the period with $263,515.35, more than Foulkes. He raised $14,709 from individuals, no PAC or party money, and lent his campaign another $20,000 (a loan from Kenneth J. Block received Oct. 21). He spent $234,138.39, mostly $186,550 in TV and other advertising through Onvision Media plus polling and ad production. He ended with $64,085.96, a 76% drop. His loans payable now total $1,269,301.60. His report lists only $675.82 in in-kind contributions.

Republican Aaron Guckian has also filed (report). He started with $7,273.99, raised $5,432.70 from individuals, lent himself $7,000, spent $16,961.69, and ended with $2,895.00. He reported no in-kind contributions.

Fact check / context: (1) This is legal and common. A state party paying for its nominee’s mailers and staff and reporting it as an in-kind contribution is lawful and routine in Rhode Island and elsewhere. Nothing in these filings suggests a violation. (2) Block is largely spending his own money. His $1,269,301.60 in loans payable is personal money, including loans from his 2010 and 2014 runs, according to earlier Rhode Island Current and Providence Journal reporting. This period his $20,000 loan was larger than the $14,709 he raised from donors. Foulkes also self-funded heavily in 2022, which is where her $1.835 million loan balance comes from. (3) Sources. Every figure here comes from the candidates’ own CF-2 filings, from the summary pages and the itemized schedules, linked below. As of publication we found no press coverage of these seven-day reports. In-kind values are what the campaign reported, not an independent audit. Independent expenditures by outside groups do not appear in candidate reports and are not counted here.

Virtue Forge commentary

Look at where each campaign’s money came from. In the final three weeks, the largest single source of support for the Democratic nominee was not any individual donor but her party. The state committee paid for about $220,000 in mailers and some staff pay, worth about half of everything she raised in cash. The independent in the race raised less than $15,000 from donors, lent himself $20,000, and spent his bank account down by three-quarters to stay on the air.

None of this breaks a rule. But it shows how Rhode Island politics works. One-party states do not need anything improper to tilt the field. The party apparatus, built up over decades and fed by the same donors, unions, and insiders year after year, simply moves its money and staff behind the nominee. A challenger outside that system has to pay for his own voice. Voters who say they want fresh competition in State House politics should notice how much of the “grassroots” campaign in their mailbox was bought by the party at 10 Weybosset Street.

Bias check: Parties are free associations, and supporting their nominees is part of what they are for. Block’s self-funding is also an advantage most citizens do not have, and Foulkes once self-funded on a far larger scale. The point is not that either campaign did something wrong. It is that voters should know who is paying for the message, and that centralized party money gives the establishment pick a structural edge that has nothing to do with the merits of her ideas.

What to watch

• Late in-kind or independent-expenditure filings in the final week, including from the party and outside groups such as the Democratic Governors Association.
• Whether Block lends his campaign more before Nov. 3.
• Post-election reports, which will show the full final-week spending.
