DCYF Freezes Admissions at Narragansett Boys’ Facility Over Safety and Supervision, Three Months After an Audit Rated Its Supervision “Exceeds Standard”

Virtue Forge briefing · Oct. 29, 2026 · Based on NBC 10 (WJAR) and Providence Journal reporting and the facility’s 2026 PREA audit report posted on DCYF’s website

What happened

The Rhode Island Department of Children, Youth and Families has stopped new intakes and referrals to Ocean Tides, a residential and educational program for at-risk boys on Ocean Road in Narragansett. According to NBC 10, the department says an investigation turned up problems with the safety and supervision of the young people there. A DCYF spokesman told the Providence Journal the investigation revealed “serious concerns regarding the safety and supervision of young people enrolled at the Narragansett facility.” The program is run by the De La Salle Christian Brothers, the Journal noted.

DCYF has put the facility’s operating license on probation. The conditions include doubling overnight staffing, and the department wants a written Plan of Corrective Action by Nov. 6. DCYF told NBC 10 it will keep monitoring and could take further action, including revoking the license.

The facility’s side

Ocean Tides President and CEO Brian Sullivan told NBC 10 the school is “taking this matter very seriously and has already taken action” and will file its plan by Nov. 6. He pointed to one resident: “We have been working with a student whose behavior is very challenging for our program, which we made DCYF aware of on September 8th. Ocean Tides is coordinating and working with DCYF while the Department seeks alternatives for this student.”

What the summer audit said

Ocean Tides is a contracted placement provider for DCYF, according to its federal Prison Rape Elimination Act (PREA) audit, a final report now posted on DCYF’s website. The on-site audit took place July 14–15, 2026, and the final report was submitted July 31. The auditor, paid through a third-party firm, Corrections Consulting Services, LLC, found Ocean Tides met 40 standards, exceeded three, and failed none. One of the three it exceeded was Standard 115.313, “Supervision and monitoring.” The report recorded 22 youth and 60 current staff on the first day. It logged staff-to-youth ratios of about 1:4 by day and 1:8 overnight during the tour, and elsewhere about 1:3 by day and 1:6 overnight. It said these practices “materially strengthen supervision beyond the juvenile PREA minimum.”

Fact check / context: (1) A PREA audit is narrow. It checks a facility’s policies and practices for preventing and responding to sexual abuse, which include staffing and supervision. It is not a full licensing review, and nothing reported so far ties DCYF’s current concerns to sexual abuse. (2) Conditions can change in three months. The audit only covers what the auditor saw and reviewed in July. (3) DCYF has not released its investigation findings or said when the investigation began or what set it off. Sullivan’s statement points to a single difficult student, but that is the facility’s account, and DCYF has not confirmed it. (4) We could not read the full Providence Journal story, which is paywalled. Our quotes from it come from its public summary.

Virtue Forge commentary

These boys are in the state’s hands. Ocean Tides is a DCYF-contracted placement provider, and DCYF licenses the program and refers youth to it. When the state takes custody of a child, it owes that child safety and competent supervision. So it is fair to ask how a facility could be rated as going beyond the supervision standard in July and then, by late October, be found by its own regulator to have “serious concerns” about supervision, serious enough to order overnight staffing doubled.

There are two uncomfortable possibilities, and the public deserves to know which is true. Either things fell apart quickly over the summer and fall, or the compliance paperwork, built on policy reviews, questionnaires, and a two-day site visit, was never a good gauge of what happens on the overnight shift. The facility’s own account raises a third question. If it warned DCYF about a student it could not safely handle on Sept. 8, why is the department, seven weeks later, still “seeking alternatives”? If the department cannot find a suitable placement for a high-need youth in seven weeks, that is a state problem, not just a provider problem.

DCYF deserves credit for acting and for posting the audit where anyone can read it. The next step is to publish what its investigators actually found, so parents, judges, and lawmakers can compare it with the clean report card from July.

Bias check: Virtue Forge is skeptical of state agency performance and of box-checking oversight. To be fair, Ocean Tides has not been found to have harmed any child, its license is on probation rather than revoked, and it says it is cooperating. One hard-to-place resident can strain any small program. The point here is not to convict the facility. It is that the state’s systems for vetting and monitoring the places it sends children should not give a top supervision rating three months before a supervision crisis.

What to watch

• Ocean Tides’ corrective action plan, due Nov. 6, and whether DCYF accepts it or moves toward revoking the license.
• Whether DCYF or the Office of the Child Advocate releases the investigation’s findings.
• Where DCYF places the student Ocean Tides flagged on Sept. 8, and how long it took.
