Rhode Island

Outgoing AG Neronha’s Parting Plan: A Roughly $400M-a-Year State-Run Insurance Plan and a Health Super-Agency to “Directly Control Health Care Financing” in Rhode Island

Virtue Forge briefing · Oct. 29, 2026 · Based on the Rhode Island Attorney General’s Oct. 29 announcement and Providence Journal coverage of the proposal

What Neronha proposed

On Oct. 29, Attorney General Peter Neronha, a Democrat who leaves office in January because of term limits, released what his office called the “final health care proposals of his administration.” There are two. The first would reorganize Rhode Island’s health and human services agencies. The second would create a “comprehensive public health insurance option,” a state-managed plan sold on and off the HealthSource RI marketplace that any individual could buy and any employer could adopt. According to the AG’s press release, the plan would “mirror the state employee health plan” and would give the state “a permanent vehicle … to directly control health care financing in the state.”

The price tag

The Providence Journal reports that, according to the proposal, the public option would need close to $400 million a year in state funding, which the proposal puts at about 3% of the state’s total annual health care spending. Under the plan the state would collect premiums, pay providers, and use state subsidies to make coverage cheaper. The design comes from work with Brown University’s Center for Advancing Health Policy through Research. That group’s earlier policy brief for the AG said a comprehensive public option would have the state “set payment rates” for providers and “could create a glide-path toward a single-payer system.”

The reorganization

The plan would split the current health and human services structure into two cabinet-level agencies. A Health Administration and Financing Agency would take over the Department of Health, Medicaid, the behavioral health and developmental disabilities department (BHDDH), the Office of the Health Insurance Commissioner, and HealthSource RI. A Human Services Agency would take over the Department of Human Services, the Office of Healthy Aging, and the Department of Children, Youth and Families. A new Office of Health Policy and Planning would sit alongside them to collect data and “steer strategic health policymaking in the executive branch.” The AG’s release says the goal is to “centralize all agencies with a role in health care regulation.”

Neronha’s case is that the current system is failing. His office says more than 25% of Rhode Islanders are underinsured, that federal changes are expected to cost 52,000 residents their coverage, and that insurers keep winning approval for steep premium increases over his objections. “I understand some will scoff at the size and scope of these proposals,” he said. He urged state leaders to “implement them after I leave office.”

Fact check: (1) These are proposals, not law, and no bill implementing them has been filed. The General Assembly would have to act after Neronha is gone. The AG is taking written public comment at healthcare@riag.ri.gov until Nov. 30. (2) The roughly $400 million figure is the proposal’s own estimate as reported by the Providence Journal; Virtue Forge has not independently checked it. The underinsurance and coverage-loss numbers are also the AG’s figures. (3) The two states that have run public options so far have mixed records. In Washington, a peer-reviewed study found only 1% of marketplace enrollees chose the public option in its first year and its plans averaged just over 11% more than the cheapest silver plan. A 2023 Washington state report later found public-option premiums fell 6% from 2021 to 2023 while other marketplace premiums rose 15%. In Colorado, a 2025 study found benchmark silver premiums rose more after the Colorado Option than in comparison states, making coverage cheaper for subsidized buyers but costlier for unsubsidized ones. Rhode Island’s version would go further than either, since it would be open to employers of every size.

Virtue Forge commentary

Neronha is right that Rhode Island health care is in rough shape. Primary care is hard to find, premiums keep climbing, and hospitals are on shaky ground. Our pages have covered the CharterCARE rescue and the state’s own struggles to run agencies like DCYF and BHDDH. But his answer is to hand the same state government more power: one super-agency over regulation, a new planning office to “steer” policy, and a state-run insurer that sets what doctors get paid, funded with roughly $400 million a year. The release says the quiet part plainly. The point is for the state to “directly control health care financing.”

Rhode Island already tried consolidation. The Executive Office of Health and Human Services was created in 2005 as an umbrella over these departments, and the proposal itself says it ended up delegating functions “without sufficient monitoring.” Another reorganization chart does not fix that. And a public option that pays providers less than private plans, in a state already short of primary care doctors, risks pushing more of them out. Before Rhode Island commits $400 million a year to a state insurer, it should ask why the agencies it already has cannot deliver, and look at cheaper fixes: more competition among insurers, scope-of-practice reform, fewer certificate-of-need barriers, and price transparency.

Bias check: Virtue Forge favors market competition over state-run insurance, and we have been critical of state agency performance. Public-option supporters argue a state plan adds competition rather than replacing it, and Washington’s later premium numbers give them some support. Neronha’s critique of the current agencies is also shared by people across the spectrum. Our objection is to the remedy, not the diagnosis.

What to watch

• Whether the next attorney general, the next governor, or any legislative leader picks the proposals up after the Nov. 3 election.
• Whether a bill is drafted for the 2027 session, and what it says about funding the roughly $400 million.
• Public comments due to the AG by Nov. 30.
