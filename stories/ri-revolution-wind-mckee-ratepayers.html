Rhode Island

Locked In for 20 Years: McKee Calls Revolution Wind a “Major Win” as Households Face a $31 Winter Default-Rate Jump

Virtue Forge briefing · Nov. 6, 2026 · Based on UpriseRI, the Governor’s Office, and the Office of Energy Resources

What happened

Ørsted said in its Nov. 5, 2026 third-quarter interim report that the 704-megawatt Revolution Wind farm reached commercial operations in October, UpriseRI reports (Adriana Vossari, Nov. 6). Rhode Island Energy holds a 20-year contract for 400 MW of the farm’s output; Connecticut utilities hold the remaining 304 MW, according to a Power Advisory assessment prepared for the Rhode Island Office of Energy Resources.

Gov. Dan McKee, in a Nov. 5 statement, said the project had reached “100% operational status,” called it a “major win for Rhode Island’s workforce and our state’s energy future,” and said helping bring it to completion had been “a highlight of my administration,” including work he described as protecting it from federal interference. McKee lost the Sept. 9 Democratic primary to Helena Foulkes; the statement lands as an exit-lap claim about a long-term energy deal.

The Rhode Island contract sets a flat wholesale price of $98.425 per megawatt-hour, or about 9.8¢/kWh, that does not rise over the 20-year term, the Power Advisory report states. That is what the utility pays for the power—not the rate on a household bill. The same consultant assessment, prepared for the state, projects $374 million in energy and renewable-energy-certificate savings over the contract life plus $139 million from lower wholesale energy prices, and a residential bill reduction of $4 to $10 in the first full year. Those figures are consultant estimates for the state, not savings already realized. The Office of Energy Resources repeated the $374 million / $139 million projections in its own Nov. 5 statement.

Separately—and not as a claim that Revolution Wind alone caused this—Rhode Island Energy’s July 22 filing asked the Public Utilities Commission to approve a winter Last Resort Service default supply rate of 17.029¢/kWh starting Oct. 1, versus a summer rate of 11.092¢. The filing estimated the change would add $30.93 a month, or 21.4%, for a typical customer using 500 kWh, per UpriseRI.

The farm sits about 15 nautical miles southeast of Point Judith and has 65 turbines, per the Bureau of Ocean Energy Management. It began delivering power in March; the final turbine went up in September. Home-count claims vary: BOEM’s project page says nearly 250,000 homes; the developer says the equivalent of more than 350,000 New England homes. Two prior federal stop-work orders (Aug. 22 and Dec. 22, 2025) were blocked in court; on Oct. 9 Interior asked the court to remand the 2023 construction-and-operations-plan approval—still pending as of Nov. 5, per UpriseRI. Green Oceans president Michael Lombardi criticized the project as harming fishermen and the environment.

Fact check: (1) Commercial-operations claim is Ørsted’s, from its Nov. 5 Q3 interim report, as reported by UpriseRI—not an independent meter reading published here. (2) The $98.425/MWh price, 400 MW RI / 304 MW CT split, and $374M / $139M / $4–$10 projections come from the Power Advisory assessment for RI OER; label them as consultant estimates prepared for the state, not cash already in ratepayer pockets. (3) The ~$30.93 winter default-rate jump is from RI Energy’s July 22 Last Resort Service filing (17.029¢ vs 11.092¢ for a 500 kWh customer). Do not claim Revolution Wind caused that winter spike; the stories are juxtaposed as timing and ratepayer context. (4) Home-count figures disagree (BOEM ~250k vs developer >350k); we note the variance. (5) Federal stop-work history and Interior’s pending remand request are background, not the lead.

Virtue Forge commentary

A governor who lost his primary still gets a victory lap when a 20-year power purchase locks in. McKee calls Revolution Wind a “major win” and a highlight of his administration. Ratepayers get a flat wholesale PPA at about 9.8¢/kWh for two decades—plus consultant forecasts of future savings—while the same season’s default supply rate jumps roughly $31 a month for a typical 500 kWh household. Future modeled savings and today’s Last Resort Service spike are not the same ledger line. Treating a long-term offshore contract as proof that households are better off now is politics, not a bill.

Free enterprise expands reliable supply and lets customers see tradeoffs. A 20-year state-backed PPA is a political bet that consultants’ net-present-value math will outrun scarcity, transmission constraints, and whatever else drives New England wholesale prices. Humble government would separate the ribbon-cutting from the winter default-rate filing, publish what households actually pay month to month, and stop selling projected savings as already-banked relief. Locked-in contracts and exit-lap press releases do not repeal the price signal on the October bill.

Bias check: Virtue Forge favors competitive markets and is skeptical of multi-decade political energy bets sold as automatic household wins. To be fair: the PPA’s flat $98.425/MWh wholesale price does not escalate over 20 years; Power Advisory and OER project net savings if those forecasts hold; and we are not asserting that Revolution Wind caused RI Energy’s winter Last Resort Service increase. The news is the juxtaposition—McKee’s “major win” framing after a primary loss, a locked 20-year contract, consultant savings estimates, and a separate ~$31 winter default-rate jump already on the books.

What to watch

• Whether actual first-year residential bills show anything like the consultant $4–$10 projected reduction.
• Rhode Island Energy’s next default-rate filing (rates beginning April 1, 2027), due in January per the utility’s filing.
• Judge Lamberth’s decision on Interior’s Oct. 9 request to remand the 2023 COP approval.
• PUC docket activity on the winter Last Resort Service rate and EmpowerRI shopping options for supply.
