Rhode Island’s Own Environmental Agency Now Says Its “Enforceable” Act on Climate Mandate Is “No Longer Realistic”

Virtue Forge briefing · Nov. 14, 2026 · Based on WPRI 12 (Nesi’s Notes, Nov. 14, 2026), the R.I. Department of Environmental Management, the draft 2025 Climate Action Strategy and R.I. Gen. Laws chapter 42-6.2

What happened

In his Saturday column, WPRI’s Ted Nesi reported that briefing material for Gov.-elect Helena Foulkes shows the state’s environmental agency is now telling the incoming administration that the climate law’s targets can’t be met: “Documents provided to the transition show RIDOT has paused efforts to award a contract to resume truck tolls until Foulkes makes a final decision, and DEM has warned the state’s Act on Climate goals are “no longer realistic.””

The 2021 Act on Climate is not a set of hopes. It writes greenhouse-gas cuts into state law: 45% below 1990 levels by 2030, 80% by 2040, and net-zero by 2050. DEM’s own inventory page calls them “mandatory, enforceable, greenhouse gas emissions reduction mandates.”

And they really are enforceable. Under R.I. Gen. Laws § 42-6.2-10, the attorney general, “any Rhode Island resident,” or any registered Rhode Island organization may sue in Superior Court for an injunction, a declaratory judgment or a writ of mandamus to force compliance with the climate plans or the emissions mandate. Courts can award attorney and expert-witness fees to “any substantially prevailing party.” The law barred those suits before 2026. That window is now open.

A year ago the state was saying something very different. The draft 2025 Climate Action Strategy (“Draft V3,” released for public comment in November 2025) said that “if current policies and programs are successfully implemented, Rhode Island is on track to meet its 2030 emissions reduction target of 45% below 1990 levels.” Its own sensitivity run, without the California-style vehicle rules, came out at 43% by 2030. The biggest drivers it listed were state programs: the Renewable Energy Standard, the state energy-efficiency program, the DRIVE EV rebates, Clean Heat Rhode Island, and the California-derived ACC II and ACT vehicle rules.

When the climate council, chaired by DEM Director Terry Gray, approved the final strategy in December 2025, the tone had shifted. DEM’s release said that carrying out these initiatives “as originally planned would’ve put the state on track,” but that federal tariffs, the end of IRA clean-energy and EV tax credits, the rollback of the vehicle waivers, offshore-wind delays and the end of Solar for All “significantly altered the landscape.” It said the council was “evaluating alternative paths to achieve meaningful emissions reductions while protecting ratepayers from unsustainable costs.”

What we haven’t seen: The transition documents Nesi describes are not public, and WPRI quoted only the two words “no longer realistic.” We don’t know which targets DEM means (2030 only, or 2040 and 2050 too), what numbers it used, or what changes it recommends. We are not putting words in DEM’s mouth beyond what was reported. If the documents come out, we’ll update this page.
Fair context: DEM’s explanation is federal policy, and some of that is real. Lost federal tax credits and vehicle-rule rollbacks do change the math. But the mandate was always a statute with dates and lawsuits attached, written as if the state controlled federal policy, the regional grid, interest rates and what cars and furnaces Rhode Islanders choose to buy. It doesn’t. Neither does any state.

Virtue Forge commentary

This is what central planning by statute looks like when it runs into the real world. In 2021 the General Assembly didn’t pass goals. It passed mandates, with a built-in right for any resident or activist group to haul the state into court, and a fee-shifting clause to pay their lawyers. Five years later, the agency in charge of hitting those mandates is quietly telling the incoming governor they can’t be hit.

A year ago the official line was “on track.” Now it’s “no longer realistic.” Rhode Islanders deserve to know which one is true, and why the honest assessment showed up in a private transition binder instead of a public report.

The danger now is that the mandate stays on the books while the targets slip. That would hand energy policy to whoever files the first lawsuit, with a judge deciding how fast Rhode Islanders must give up their furnaces and gas cars, and with ratepayers and taxpayers covering the bill. DEM itself says ratepayers need protection from “unsustainable costs.” The responsible fix is for the General Assembly to revisit the law: drop the hard deadlines and the litigation trigger, and let energy choices follow cost and technology instead of a statute’s calendar.

Bias check: Coverage of the Act on Climate has mostly asked how to save the targets: more subsidies, more mandates, more spending. Almost no one asks the question the law’s design forces: what happens to Rhode Island families when an unrealistic target is enforced in court? Nesi deserves credit for surfacing the admission. It deserves a lot more attention than one sentence in a weekend column.

What to watch: whether Foulkes’s team releases the DEM transition material; any 60-day notice of suit under § 42-6.2-10; any 2027 bill to amend the mandates; and the truck-toll decision RIDOT is waiting on.
