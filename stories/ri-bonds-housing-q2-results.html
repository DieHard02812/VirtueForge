Rhode Island

Voters Pass the $120 Million Housing Bond. They Also Reject Two Bonds, the First Defeats Since 2006, Including $100 Million With “No Specific Projects” Named

Virtue Forge briefing · Nov. 4, 2026 · Based on Rhode Island Current’s report of unofficial Board of Elections results, Ocean State Media, and What’s Up Newp. All results are unofficial.

What happened

These results are unofficial. The Board of Elections has not certified them. Rhode Island Current reports these figures from the board’s unofficial results as of 10:19 p.m. Tuesday:

• Question 1, higher education, $275 million: 59.82% approved.
• Question 2, housing, $120 million: 61.12% approved.
• Question 3, economic development, $100 million: 52.47% rejected.
• Question 4, cultural economy, $50 million: 50.79% rejected.
• Question 5, green economy, $55 million: 66.92% approved.

Ocean State Media, writing shortly after midnight, also had Questions 1, 2 and 5 “on track to pass.” It said Questions 3 and 4 “appeared to be more closely contested as votes continued to be counted.”

The defeats matter beyond the totals. Rhode Island Current calls them “the first time voters in the Ocean State have defeated a bond measure since 2006, when they rejected a $4 million bond for Newport’s Fort Adams.” About the $100 million economic-development bond, the outlet writes: “No specific projects were earmarked for the funding, however.” It also notes the measure “was a scaled-down version of Gov. Dan McKee’s original $115 million proposal.” The $50 million cultural bond would mostly have paid for a State History Center, Secretary of State Gregg Amore’s longstanding goal.

The housing bond and its track record

Question 2 repeats the $120 million housing bond voters approved in 2024. At least $25 million goes to homeownership housing, and up to $10 million can support public housing. Projects are expected to start in 2028, according to Rhode Island Current. The outlet adds that the approval “comes amid scrutiny of the effectiveness of previous state investments in housing.” A May report by the Rhode Island Public Expenditure Council “found that $52.2 million in public funding had financed 200 new rental units, for an average development cost of more than $512,000 per unit.” State housing officials disagreed. Housing Secretary Deborah Goddard “said in October that the state’s actual subsidy per unit was much lower.”

Context: the award announced five days before the vote

On Thursday, Oct. 29, McKee announced a $3,239,964 award for Aquidneck Green in Middletown, What’s Up Newp reported. It was the largest award in the round. Marathon Development will build 36 modular three-bedroom homes on town-owned land. That comes to roughly $90,000 in state money per home, our calculation, with sale prices of $353,800 to $399,999. The money came from the 2024 housing bond. The outlet reported that the announcement “came five days before Rhode Island voters decide on a new housing bond, and both McKee and Secretary of Housing Deborah Goddard used the event to campaign for it.” McKee said: “I urge Rhode Islanders to help us keep this momentum going by voting ‘Yes’ on Question 2 on November 3.”

Fact check: (1) All figures are unofficial. Questions 3 and 4 were still described as close at midnight, and Question 4’s margin was under two points. Mail ballots left in drop boxes on Election Day and military and overseas ballots are expected to be tallied by Nov. 10, and certification is expected before Thanksgiving, Rhode Island Current reports. The board’s live statewide results are here. (2) The roughly $90,000 per home is the award divided by 36 homes, and it is state subsidy only, not the full cost of building a home. (3) The Middletown award is context, not evidence of wrongdoing. The event was a lawful official announcement, and we know of no rule it broke. (4) The $512,000 figure is RIPEC’s analysis, which state housing officials dispute.

Virtue Forge commentary

For two decades, Rhode Island bond questions passed as a formality. On Tuesday voters drew a line. They turned down a $100 million economic-development fund that named no specific projects, and a $50 million package built mostly around a new state building. They still approved borrowing they could tie to concrete purposes: campuses, housing and parks. A request to “trust us” with $100 million didn’t pass. That is a healthy instinct.

The housing bond passed easily, and the state now owes voters results it can measure. The governor used a public award announcement, paid for by the last housing bond, as a stage to campaign for the next one. That is legal. It makes it all the more important to report what each dollar produces. RIPEC’s $512,000 figure for subsidized rental units and the state’s rebuttal should be settled with public, unit-by-unit numbers before the new money goes out.

Bias check: Virtue Forge is skeptical of state borrowing without specifics, and we read the defeats of Questions 3 and 4 favorably. To be fair, history-center backers argued that Rhode Island is the only state without such a facility. The roughly $90,000 per Middletown home is a subsidy figure and can’t be compared directly with RIPEC’s per-unit development cost. Approval of a bond does not mean borrowing starts immediately.

What to watch

• Certified results, especially Question 4.
• Whether McKee or the Assembly brings the economic-development and history-center money back by another route, such as the budget or a future ballot.
• How the Executive Office of Housing reports per-unit costs for Question 2 money.
• Aquidneck Green’s construction and sales.
