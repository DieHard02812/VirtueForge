Blindfold by Ordinance: Providence Council Moves to Ban Flock—and Bar Cops From Using Anyone Else’s Plate Data

Virtue Forge briefing · Based on WPRI / Target 12 (Oct. 13–15, 2026), Providence Journal (Oct. 14, 2026), and WJAR (Oct. 15, 2026); updated Oct. 16 with WPRI’s report on the council meeting

What happened

Providence’s City Council is advancing an ordinance to prohibit automated license-plate readers (ALPRs) and similar technology citywide—not merely to let a vendor contract lapse. According to the Providence Journal (Oct. 14), sponsors include Councilors Miguel Sanchez and Sue Anderbois and outgoing Council President Rachel Miller. The draft, as described in Journal and WPRI coverage, would immediately deactivate and discontinue LPR agreements, return the equipment, and bar the city from receiving LPR data from other states, municipalities, or agencies.

WPRI / Target 12 reported that Thursday’s council agenda presented an amendment citing constituent concerns about misuse elsewhere—including alleged Immigration and Customs Enforcement use of Flock data, stalking by authorized users, and misidentification. Police Col. Oscar Perez told Target 12 the move surprised him and that he would have welcomed operational input before language affecting investigations was drafted. The department oversees 60 city Flock cameras; Rhode Island State Police oversee six more, per police.

Mayor Brett Smiley defended the cameras as a proven public-safety tool and urged guardrails over elimination. Democratic mayoral nominee David Morales’s campaign manager said Morales intends to cancel Flock once in office and “fully support[s]” the council ordinance. Republican mayoral nominee David Talan called the cameras valuable if data-sharing can be locked down.

Fact check: Earlier public reporting (and our September briefing) treated Providence’s Flock contract as expiring around October 2026. On Thursday, police spokesperson Kristy DosReis told WPRI that after reviewing the contract, the city confirmed expiration is tied to camera installation dates and is May 16, 2027—not Oct. 31. Do not write that the program “dies this month” unless the ordinance passes and forces deactivation sooner. WJAR reported introduction without an immediate final vote; committee process still applies. Privacy critiques (ACLU, other RI towns canceling Flock, McKee’s August ALPR review pause) are real parts of the debate—cite them as debate, not as proof every Providence hit was a false positive.

Update (Oct 16)

The council did not vote on the ban Thursday night. According to WPRI, “Councilors did not vote on the proposal, but did discuss it at length Thursday night. It was referred to committee.” WPRI again gives the city’s Flock contract expiration as May 16, 2027.

Councilor Sue AnderBois, a sponsor, told the council: “Flock and other license plate reader technologies are not keeping us safer. Data from Providence’s Flock cameras has been accessed millions of times — including by many organizations that do not have the required formal sharing agreements with the Providence Police Department.” She added: “This type of surveillance data is being weaponized across the country, and constant monitoring is an invasion of our civil liberties.”

In a statement to WPRI, Flock Safety said it hopes “city leaders recognize the impact this technology has already had on public safety,” adding: “Most notably, Flock technology played a critical role in helping law enforcement identify and locate the suspect responsible for the deadly Brown University shooting.”

Fact check: “Referred to committee” means the ordinance is neither passed nor dead. It needs committee action before any council vote. AnderBois’s claim that Providence’s Flock data “has been accessed millions of times,” including by organizations without formal sharing agreements, is her assertion as reported by WPRI. Virtue Forge has not seen the underlying audit data, and the police department has not publicly confirmed it. If it’s accurate, it’s an argument for enforcing the sharing rules, which the city says already exist, not proof the cameras don’t work. Correction: an earlier version of this page called Morales “mayor-elect.” He is the Democratic nominee; the election is Nov. 3.

Virtue Forge commentary

This is no longer just a campaign applause line. The same progressive council faction that has already practiced cancel-first governance on rent control is trying to legislate the blindfold before the DSA-aligned Democratic nominee for mayor even has the keys—and to wall Providence off from plate data other departments might share. That is a competence choice dressed up as civil-liberties theater: not “audit the sharing settings,” but “darken the cameras and refuse the feed.”

Police say the network has been instrumental in major cases, including the Brown University shooting vehicle track. Cranston’s Flock hit on an out-of-state cooking-oil theft ring earlier this fall showed what a timely plate flag looks like on a boring overnight job. Humble government can demand warrants, retention limits, and public audit logs. It does not owe activist forums a city that cannot see a license plate leaving a homicide scene—or borrow a hit from Warwick when a van rolls across the city line.

Bias check: Warrantless bulk surveillance deserves skepticism; several Rhode Island towns have paused or canceled Flock for privacy reasons, and McKee ordered a state review of new installs. Those facts cut against a cartoon “cops good / activists bad” frame. The point here is narrower: an ordinance that bans the tool and blocks outside LPR data is a harder public-safety downgrade than tightening contracts. Smiley’s “guardrails” line and Talan’s conditional keep are the free-enterprise / competent-policing middle. Morales’s camp cheering a total ban is the municipal-socialist endgame voters already sampled in the primary.

What to watch: whether Thursday’s introduction advances out of committee before Nov. 3; whether Perez gets a formal hearing; and whether the May 2027 contract date survives political pressure to deactivate “immediately” on paper.
