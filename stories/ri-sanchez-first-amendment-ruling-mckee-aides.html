Federal Judge Rules McKee’s Top Two Aides Violated a State Worker’s First Amendment Rights by Firing Him Over an Off-Duty Post, Denies Them Qualified Immunity

Virtue Forge briefing · Oct. 29, 2026 · Based on the Memorandum and Order in Sanchez v. Almond, No. 24-cv-319-JJM-RLA (D.R.I. Oct. 28, 2026), with coverage from GoLocalProv and Rhode Island Current (Oct. 28)

What the court decided

On Oct. 28, U.S. District Court Chief Judge John J. McConnell Jr. ruled that Gov. Dan McKee’s chief of staff, Antonio Afonso, and senior deputy chief of staff, Joseph Almond, violated the First Amendment when they fired Miguel Sanchez, a junior constituent-services staffer, in October 2023 over a post on his personal social media account. In the 25-page memorandum and order, the judge granted Sanchez partial summary judgment “as to liability on his First Amendment claim against Defendants Antonio Afonso and Joseph Almond in their individual capacities and against all Defendants in their official capacities.” He denied the defendants’ motion, including their claim of qualified immunity. “The matter of damages and injunctive relief remains.”

The speech, in one line. While off duty, Sanchez, who was also a Providence city councilor, posted on X a video of a Providence march calling for a ceasefire in the Israel–Hamas war. His account identified him as a councilor, not a state employee.

“Constitutional right to say” it, then fired

According to the ruling, at an Oct. 26, 2023 meeting Afonso told Sanchez that he “ha[d] the constitutional right to say” whatever he wanted but “doesn’t have any constitutional right to work in the Governor’s office.” The next day, Sanchez “was given the choice of resigning or being fired,” and “[h]is request to speak with Human Resources was denied.” Afonso testified that if Sanchez had offered to remove the post, he “might have” kept his job.

The judge rejected the idea that a government job can be taken away for any reason because it is a privilege. In a footnote he wrote: “There is an incorrect notion that because someone may not have a right to a government benefit, such as public employment, the benefit may be denied for any reason.”

The “disruption” defense

The defendants argued that the firing was justified by disruption to the office. The court counted what that disruption was in the five days between the post and the firing: one Providence Journal inquiry, “three emails and two telephone messages from the public,” a legislator’s question in an elevator, and a few questions from NBC 10 and a WPRO host. It also credited testimony that the governor was asked about it at press gaggles. Almond himself testified the inquiries were not “a lot” and “did not interfere with the operation of the office.”

The firing, by contrast, drew at least 17 reporters’ questions, coverage from at least 11 outlets, and 63 emails and 38 calls by midday Nov. 2. “The alleged distraction caused by his post pales in comparison to the inquiries after the firing,” McConnell wrote.

The court also noted that two other staffers had posted views that conflicted with the governor’s. One was a policy staffer who called for abolishing the Law Enforcement Officers’ Bill of Rights, and the other was a scheduler who backed a different presidential candidate. “[N]either employee was asked to remove the posts or was fired.” The judge also found that Sanchez “was not a policymaker or communicator as a matter of law.” He concluded: “Defendants’ disagreement with Mr. Sanchez’s post places nothing legitimate on their side of the balancing scale.”

Why qualified immunity failed

Qualified immunity shields officials from personal damages unless they violated a clearly established right. McConnell held that the law was clear and that the aides’ own words showed they knew it: “Both the Chief of Staff and the Deputy Chief of Staff of the Governor knew or should have known that they should not retaliate against Mr. Sanchez for exercising his constitutional right. Yet the following day Mr. Sanchez was terminated from his employment right after Mr. Afonso acknowledged that Mr. Sanchez had a right to express himself.” He cited First Circuit precedent that denied qualified immunity to a governor who removed an official over his vote on a matter of public concern.

The court ruled for the defendants on one claim, involving a job Sanchez applied for at the Department of Labor and Training, finding no evidence of an adverse action or of a causal link to the governor’s staff.

Fact check: (1) This is not a final judgment. The court decided liability on the First Amendment claim. Damages, which are left to the trier of fact, and Sanchez’s request to be reinstated are still open, and the case continues. (2) An appeal is possible. Denials of qualified immunity can often be appealed before final judgment. McKee spokesperson Olivia DaRocha told Rhode Island Current that the governor’s office disagreed with the decision and was “reviewing all options with legal counsel.” (3) Rhode Island Current reports that the aides are represented by the Attorney General’s office and that Almond and Afonso still work for McKee, whose term ends in January. (4) The ruling was first reported by GoLocalProv. All quotations above are from the court’s order unless otherwise attributed.

Virtue Forge commentary

This ruling is about power, not about anyone’s views on a foreign war. The two most powerful unelected people in the governor’s office told a junior employee he had a constitutional right to speak, then made him choose between resigning and being fired the next day. Afterward, the office told the press his words had become “a major distraction.” By the court’s count, the “distraction” before the firing was a handful of emails and calls. The other staffers whose posts broke with the governor kept their jobs.

Free speech protections exist for exactly this case: officials at the top deciding which opinions their employees may hold on their own time. If the governor’s office can do this to a city councilor working a constituent-services desk, it can do it to anyone whose politics are inconvenient that week. And taxpayers are paying for the defense, through the Attorney General’s office.

Bias check: Virtue Forge has been critical of Sanchez’s politics (see our earlier brief on his bid for council president), and we take no side here on the underlying Middle East debate. The principle has to apply regardless of whose speech is punished. The court’s findings rest on the summary-judgment record, and the defendants dispute how disruptive the episode was and how Sanchez’s job should be characterized. An appeals court could see it differently.

What to watch

• Whether the governor’s office or the Attorney General appeals the qualified-immunity denial, and on what timeline.
• Proceedings on damages and on Sanchez’s request for reinstatement, which will now play out after McKee leaves office in January.
• Whether Almond and Afonso remain in their posts through the transition.
