Rhode Island

“Not Likely to Imperil”: Five Months After Outpatient Clearance, a Repeat Child-Stabbing Suspect Is Charged With Stabbing a Toddler in a Providence Stroller

Virtue Forge briefing · Based on Boston Globe / WPRI pool coverage (Oct. 8–9, 2026), NBC10 I-Team court records (Oct. 8), Providence Police briefings, and Attorney General Peter Neronha’s office statements on prior competency findings

What happened

On Wednesday, Oct. 7, 2026, around 1:50 p.m., Providence police say Nelson Blanco, 46, approached a 26-year-old mother waiting for her older children at a school bus stop near Broad and Public streets. The mother’s 2-year-old daughter was in a stroller. Chief Oscar Perez said Blanco insulted the mother, she crossed the street, and he approached again—then stabbed the toddler multiple times in the face (and the mother in the shoulder) in an unprovoked attack. Bystanders intervened; one chased Blanco as he broke into a Daboll Street home and locked himself in the basement. Officers took him into custody. A bloodied knife was later found in a panel box there, prosecutors said.

The child was rushed to Hasbro Children’s Hospital in critical condition—Perez said she was “fighting for her life.” The mother was treated and released. Blanco faces two counts of assault with intent to commit murder and related charges. In a police interview he admitted the attack; no motive was given. At arraignment Thursday he wore a hazmat suit; the court ordered a new competency evaluation and a dangerousness assessment. Bond was set at $200,000.

This was not a first brush with children or with Rhode Island’s competency machinery. In 2003, Blanco was charged with felony assault after stabbing two 7-year-old boys on Corinth Street in an attack police then called random and senseless. Court records obtained by the NBC10 I-Team show he was sent to Eleanor Slater Hospital—the state’s long-term psychiatric hospital, under BHDDH—after being deemed incompetent. Psychiatric reports from 2003–2006 described him as paranoid and psychotic; doctors repeatedly rated him at “moderate risk of creating a likelihood of serious harm to others.” In 2007, the state’s behavioral-health agency concluded there was “no reasonable likelihood” he would become competent in the foreseeable future, and the criminal case ended. BHDDH has declined to say when or why he left Eleanor Slater, citing patient privacy.

Attorney General Peter Neronha’s office says Blanco was again found incompetent in 2018 (stolen-vehicle case; doctors cited intellectual disability and schizophrenia) and again in July 2026 on a 2024 domestic-violence case. That July, a doctor recommended outpatient status, writing he was “not likely to imperil the peace and safety of the community or himself at this time.” Judge Brian Stern accepted the recommendation—about five months before the Broad Street attack—leaving Blanco free on the street. NBC10 notes the July order stemmed from the North Providence domestic-violence matter; some local reports also place a July 2025 BHDDH-custody release in the timeline.

Asked whether the system is broken, Perez said he does not think so—police collect facts and present cases; courts decide. Witness Jesse Alice Palmer, who helped treat the toddler at the scene, was blunt: “He should not have been out in the streets… The system failed everybody.”

Fact check: Blanco is charged, not convicted, in the Oct. 7 attack; he has pleaded to no charge in that case yet. The 2003 child-stabbing case never reached a verdict because of serial incompetency findings. The July 2026 “not likely to imperil” language is from a doctor’s recommendation that Judge Stern accepted for outpatient release—it is not a finding that Blanco was cured, nor proof that any one official knew he would attack again. Separately, this week’s Auditor General report faulted BHDDH opioid-clinic oversight (Virtue Forge #53); that audit is about Medicaid billing and clinic paperwork, not Eleanor Slater discharge decisions, and should not be conflated with this case.

Virtue Forge commentary

Protecting children from known, repeat, random violence is among the few jobs of government that almost everyone still agrees on. Rhode Island’s record here is a competence failure with a paper trail. A man accused of stabbing two seven-year-olds was warehoused as incompetent, rated a moderate risk of serious harm, declared unlikely ever to become fit for trial—and years later was back on Broad Street after a court accepted an outpatient assurance that he was “not likely to imperil” anyone. Five months after that clearance, prosecutors say he stabbed a toddler in a stroller.

That is not a mystery of street crime. It is a revolving door built by statutes, doctors, judges, and a behavioral-health apparatus that can neither keep a dangerous defendant confined nor make him competent enough to face a jury. When the chief of police shrugs that the system is not broken while a bystander who performed CPR on a two-year-old says it failed everybody, the public should believe the bystander. Process theater—competency orders, outpatient recommendations, privacy walls around Eleanor Slater discharges—is not the same as keeping knives out of toddlers’ faces.

Bias check: Serious mental illness is real, and involuntary confinement is a grave power. The critique is not that every incompetent defendant belongs locked away forever. It is that Rhode Island repeatedly found this particular defendant unfit, dangerous in the retrospective medical record, and still put him back on the street with a soft outpatient stamp—then a child paid the price. Do not invent a conspiracy; the courtable facts already convict the system’s judgment.

What to watch: the new competency and dangerousness assessments; whether prosecutors seek civil commitment or sustained detention if he is again found unfit; any public accounting from BHDDH on Eleanor Slater discharge standards (privacy claims notwithstanding for aggregate policy); and whether the Assembly treats revolving-door competency cases as a public-safety reform rather than another study commission.
