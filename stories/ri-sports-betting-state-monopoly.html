Rhode Island

Rhode Island Takes 51% of Every Online Sports Bet and Lets Two Companies Play. Rhode Islanders Placed 4.23 Million Bets Across the Border Instead

Virtue Forge briefing · Based on NBC 10 / WJAR (Nov. 12, 2026) and Global Gaming Insider (Nov. 2026)

What happened

On Thursday, Nov. 12, the Sports Betting Alliance, a trade group whose members include DraftKings, FanDuel, Fanatics Sportsbook and BetMGM, said its member companies recorded more than 4 million bets placed by Rhode Island accounts in Massachusetts, Connecticut and New York from July 2025 to July 2026. The group says those bets came from nearly 103,000 Rhode Island accounts. Online bettors have to be physically located in a state to place a bet there, so those wagers were made while the bettors were across the line.

The numbers are the opening shot in a new push at the State House to let more sports betting apps into Rhode Island. “Every one of the 4.23 million bets Rhode Islanders placed in Massachusetts, Connecticut and New York is revenue that could have stayed in Rhode Island,” alliance President and CEO Joe Maloney said in a statement.

How Rhode Island’s market works

When Rhode Island legalized online sports betting in 2019, the state authorized one provider: Sportsbook Rhode Island, operated by IGT. The state keeps 51% of sports betting revenue. Earlier this year the Rhode Island Lottery Commission picked Bally’s, the state’s only casino operator, to launch a second app when IGT’s exclusive contract expires later this month. NBC 10 reports that only one other company bid on that contract, and a FanDuel representative said FanDuel didn’t bid because the state’s cut was too high. A Bally’s spokeswoman said its app will launch sometime in December.

During the 2026 session, a bill to allow four to six sports betting apps passed the Senate but died in the House. Lottery regulators opposed it, calling it “financially detrimental” to the state and saying its lower state share would “significantly reduce” revenue. NBC 10 reports the Lottery also argued that the Rhode Island market can’t support more platforms than the two it has approved.

NBC 10 reported earlier this year that Rhode Island took in nearly $18 million in online sports betting revenue over a one-year period, while Massachusetts, which taxes online sports betting at 20% and has seven apps, took in $170 million over the same period. That is nearly 10 times the revenue in a state with roughly seven times the population.

Fact check: is 4.23 million bets really “lost revenue”? Not exactly. The figure is the industry’s own data, released by companies that want into the market, and it hasn’t been independently verified. Some of those bets were surely placed by Rhode Islanders who work in, commute to or travel through Massachusetts, Connecticut or New York and would have bet there anyway. A bet is also not revenue; the state collects a share of what the operator keeps after paying winners. The alliance’s estimate that expansion would bring in an additional $50 million over a decade is likewise its own projection. What is not in dispute: Rhode Island’s market has had one app since 2019, the state keeps more than half the revenue, and its per-person sports betting revenue trails its neighbor’s.

Virtue Forge commentary

Gambling isn’t a virtue, and Virtue Forge isn’t cheering for more of it. But once a state decides to legalize something, the question becomes whether government runs it as an open, competitive market or as a revenue machine for itself and a few chosen partners. Rhode Island picked the second option. It handed one company an exclusive contract, kept 51% of the take, and then picked the state’s only casino operator as the second player in a bidding process that drew one other bidder.

That is what a state-run cartel looks like. When government takes half the revenue, the product gets worse: worse odds, fewer features and fewer reasons for customers to stay. Then customers vote with their feet. If the alliance’s numbers are even close, about 103,000 Rhode Island accounts found a reason to place bets in other states rather than at home.

Bias check: The Sports Betting Alliance is not a neutral source. Its members stand to make money if Rhode Island opens up, and it wants a lower state share, not just more competition. The Lottery isn’t neutral either: it is the regulator, the market designer and the biggest beneficiary of the current setup all at once. That conflict is the real story. The agency deciding how much competition Rhode Island gets is the same agency collecting 51% of the revenue.

The House killed the expansion bill last session after it cleared the Senate. When the alliance comes back in January, House leaders should explain why a single state-picked app, followed by a second one awarded to the state’s only casino operator, serves Rhode Islanders better than open competition at a reasonable rate. If the answer is “it protects our cut,” voters should hear it said out loud.

What to watch: the Bally’s app launch in December, whether the alliance’s bill is refiled in the 2027 session, and whether the House lets it come to a vote this time.
