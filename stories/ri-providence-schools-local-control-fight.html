Seven Years Under the State, Three Months of Infighting: Providence School Board Member Demands the Superintendent Resign

Virtue Forge briefing · Based on GoLocalProv reporting (Oct. 6–7, 2026), with background from Uprise RI, NBC 10 and The Providence Journal (Sept. 2026)

What happened

On Tuesday, Oct. 6, longtime Providence School Board member Night Jean Muhingabo called on Superintendent Dr. Javier Montañez to step down “effective immediately,” GoLocalProv reported. Muhingabo cited a 15-page letter said to come from “Sixteen Current Administrators of the Providence Public School District,” which describes what its authors call “a troubling pattern of retaliation against employees who challenge senior leadership”: concerns get documented, an investigation is opened, and the employee ends up on leave, disciplined, or recommended for termination.

Muhingabo also pointed to a confidential letter he said the district’s Chief of Human Resources sent the board after she was placed on administrative leave the previous Wednesday. He asked the board to authorize a fully independent investigation into allegations of “retaliation, discrimination, racism, and an overall toxic work environment,” and asked Montañez to put Deputy Superintendent Paula Dillon on leave while it runs. His list of complaints against the superintendent goes beyond the letters: insubordination, undermining board governance, falsification of documents, his handling of a graduation-inflation controversy, and “continuing to take direction from the Commissioner of Education following the return to local control.”

Board President Ty’Relle Stephens told GoLocal he isn’t calling for Montañez to resign but said the allegations need to be investigated. The district’s communications office didn’t respond to GoLocal.

This is the second act of a fight that broke into the open last month. The district returned to city control this summer after nearly seven years of state oversight. In September, NBC 10 and The Boston Globe reported that six of the board’s 10 members wanted Stephens out as president. At the Sept. 23 board meeting, three departing senior administrators—Deputy Superintendent of Operations Zachary Scott, Dillon, and Chief of Staff Lisa DiMartino—each said Stephens’ leadership was a major reason they were leaving. Dillon cited a request for “a $10,000 charge card during a period of significant financial constraint.” DiMartino said the board’s chief legal counsel position was pitched as a cost saver but has added about $100,000 to the budget. Stephens apologized for the distraction, said he would publish a written response with emails and records, and didn’t step down. Muhingabo said at that meeting that he still supported Stephens.

Fact check: None of these allegations has been tested by an investigation, on either side. GoLocal says the 16-administrator letter was “allegedly” issued by that group, and no reporting we found names its authors. Treat it as unverified. That caution is earned: in September, NBC 10 got an email claiming to speak for former board members, one of whom said he hadn’t been involved in years, and a follow-up told outlets to disregard it. Two more details matter. Dillon has already resigned, effective in mid-October, so the demand to put her on leave would cover only her last days. And the details of the HR chief’s leave come from Muhingabo’s statement. The district hasn’t confirmed them publicly.

Virtue Forge commentary

For almost seven years, the Rhode Island Department of Education ran Providence’s schools from the top down. The elected school board was reduced to an advisory role, and the commissioner, not Providence voters, held the levers. The promise was that a strong central hand would fix a broken district and then hand back something healthy. What came back is a board at war with itself, a senior team heading for the exits, and dueling letters accusing each side of bullying the other.

Muhingabo’s complaint that the superintendent still “takes direction from the Commissioner” is the telling one. You can’t say the takeover is over if the district’s chief executive still answers to the state. Centralizing power didn’t build the local habits of governance a district needs, like clear lines between the board and staff, open agendas, and spending people can see. It let them wither. Now Providence has to relearn them in public, at students’ expense.

Bias check: This isn’t reformers against a bad superintendent, or the reverse. Muhingabo backed the board president the departing administrators blamed, as recently as last month’s meeting, so his call reads as the counterpunch in a factional fight, not as neutral oversight. Montañez was appointed during the state takeover, and Stephens is credited by some colleagues with helping win back local control. Both camps have made serious, unproven charges. GoLocal reported Muhingabo’s side in detail but couldn’t get the district’s.

The fix isn’t another round of state control. It’s accountability that starts close to home. The board should hire a truly independent investigator, one not chosen by the president, the superintendent, or RIDE, to look at both sets of allegations, and then publish the findings. It should also publish the full cost of its own legal counsel and every board spending request. Parents can judge a board that shows its work. They can’t judge one that fights through anonymous letters.
