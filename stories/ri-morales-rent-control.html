Price Caps Don’t Build Homes: Providence Hands DSA a Mandate to Cap Rents at 4%

Virtue Forge briefing · Based on Fox News / Boston Globe coverage of the Sept. 9, 2026 Providence Democratic primary

What happened

On Sept. 9, 2026, Rhode Island State Rep. David Morales, a 27-year-old democratic socialist endorsed by Sen. Bernie Sanders and the Democratic Socialists of America, defeated incumbent Providence Mayor Brett Smiley in the city’s Democratic mayoral primary. Fox News reported Morales won by about five points (roughly 52.5%–47.5%). The Boston Globe reported Smiley is the first incumbent Providence mayor to lose a primary in the city’s history.

Morales campaigned on capping annual rent increases, expanding affordable housing, and raising taxes on polluters and corporate landlords. Earlier in 2026, the Providence City Council passed a rent-stabilization ordinance that would have capped annual rent hikes at 4% for certain apartments; Smiley vetoed it in April, and an override fell one vote short in May. After Wednesday’s results, the council looks more friendly to rent control—Ward 1 councilor John Goncalves, who opposed the ordinance, lost to Miguel Martínez Youngs, a former policy staffer who helped craft it.

Morales faces Republican Dave Talan in November and is a heavy favorite in deep-blue Providence. He has pledged to deliver rent stabilization early in a mayoral term if elected.

Virtue Forge commentary

Affordable housing is a real problem. Price ceilings are a fake solution. When City Hall freezes rents below what the market clears, landlords stop investing, units get withheld or converted, maintenance slips, and the shortage the ordinance claimed to fix gets worse. That is Economics 101—not “housing justice.”

Bias check: National outlets frame Morales’s win as progressive energy and a rebuke of “big money / corporate real estate.” They underplay the tradeoff: a 4% hard cap is a political price control that punishes new supply and rewards scarcity politics. Calling rent control “stabilization” does not change the incentive structure. Also separate this story from the Foulkes–McKee governor race: same primary night, different policy stakes—here the mandate is explicitly anti-market on housing.

Humble, competent governance would expand the housing stock—zoning reform, faster permits, competing for builders—not outlaw price discovery. Free enterprise and American cities grow when capital can build. Cap the return and you get fewer homes, longer waitlists, and a political class that blames “greedy landlords” for the shortage it engineered.

Voters can fire mayors. They cannot repeal supply-and-demand. Providence is about to test which lesson it learned.
