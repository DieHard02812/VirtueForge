Licensed to Feed Your Neighbors? East Providence’s Housing Authority Sends a Lawyer to Shut Down a Residents’ Food Drive

Virtue Forge briefing · Based on NBC 10 WJAR (Ryan Medeiros, Nov. 9–10, 2026) and WPRI 12 (Nov. 10, 2026), with Rhode Island Department of Health and Rhode Island Community Food Bank guidance

What happened

Residents of City View and Goldsmith manors, two housing-authority complexes off Route 44 in East Providence, had an informal pantry in their building’s community room, according to organizer Coogan. Local social-media figure Brian Coogan, known online as “Mr. EP,” says residents asked him to help stock it, and his following generated hundreds of donations. “They said, please, you know, we have a pantry here. Not everybody in the building needs it, but a lot of people need it,” Coogan told NBC 10. He says he agreed on one condition: “As there’s no money involved, no cash, no gift cards, just food. I will help you raise it.”

Then, Coogan says, an attorney representing the East Providence Housing Authority (EPHA) called him. In a recording he shared with NBC 10, a voice says: “We need to ask you to immediately stop soliciting any more food donations,” and “By close of business tomorrow, we need them to be out of there.” WPRI reported on Nov. 10 that the temporary pantry “has to shut down.” Residents were given a single window, 9 a.m. to 1 p.m. on Tuesday, Nov. 10, to pick up the donated food from the community room.

The authority’s statement to NBC 10 called claims that food would be thrown away “100% False” and said residents had been told to come take whatever they wanted. It said “certain individuals unlawfully – not in compliance with pertinent RI laws and/or RI Department of Health requirements – took it upon themselves to solicit food and cash donations, allegedly on behalf of the East Providence Housing Authority but without the knowledge or consent” of the authority, and that the drive tied up the community room without permission. “No one disagrees with offering food to persons in need,” the statement said.

Coogan, who NBC 10 notes ran for state Senate as an independent this year, accused Mayor Bob DaSilva of blocking the drive because he “wasn’t invited.” DaSilva denied any role (“I keep my hands clear of the housing authority, other than appointing personnel to it”) but backed the rationale: “They are not an authorized food pantry. They’re not licensed to be a food pantry.” He called Coogan “a failed politician who is very adept at making up stories.” Coogan called the one-morning pickup window chaos: “Let the people who started this finish it.”

Fact check: The licensing point isn’t made up. Under state Department of Health food-service rules, an operation that routinely provides food to the public “with or without charge” needs a Food Business License. The Rhode Island Community Food Bank tells its member pantries the license is required, costs $0 for nonprofits, requires a health inspection of the space, and that at least one person complete food-safety training. But the state also shows there’s a lighter path: RIDOH’s own community-fridge guidance says that if basic rules are followed (no homemade food, intact packaging, labeled ingredients), “a food business license … may not be needed.” And the state’s 2019 Food Donation Act exists to protect good-faith donors. Neither the authority nor the mayor has said which specific rule a shelf of donated packaged food broke, or whether anyone got sick or was at risk.
Disputed: The authority says the drive solicited “cash donations”; Coogan says it was food only, with no cash or gift cards. We have not seen evidence either way. The authority says no one said the food would be thrown out; the recording NBC 10 quoted asks for the food to be “out of there” by the next day, which is a removal demand, not a statement about trashing it.

Virtue Forge commentary

Residents of a public-housing building asked their neighbors for groceries. Their neighbors delivered. The government landlord’s response was a call from a lawyer, a deadline, and a four-hour pickup window. Whatever the merits of the paperwork, that is the wrong instinct. When private charity shows up faster than the system, the first move of a public body should be “How do we get this legal by Friday?” and not “Shut it down by close of business tomorrow.”

The license the mayor invoked is free, and the state already has a lighter-touch model for community fridges. Based on the Food Bank’s published requirements, getting a pantry compliant appears to take an application, an inspection, and one trained volunteer, which the housing authority could have helped arrange. Instead, the organizers were told to stop and clear out, and the mayor’s advice was to “get the people involved who need to be involved,” in DaSilva’s words. That is how you turn a neighborly act into something that needs official sponsorship.

There is a fair point on the authority’s side: it runs the building, and it’s entitled to know who is using its community room and who is soliciting in its name. If anyone collected cash under the authority’s name, that deserves a real answer. But nobody in this story has alleged the food was unsafe. The harm here is one the residents, not the regulators, will feel: a pantry they asked for is gone.

Bias check: Coogan is a recent independent candidate with a public feud with the mayor, and DaSilva is a political opponent with an obvious interest in the dispute. Both accuse the other of playing politics. We have relied on what each side said on the record and on the state’s published food-safety guidance, not on either man’s characterization of the other.

What to watch: whether the authority or a licensed partner reopens a pantry at City View and Goldsmith manors, and whether the authority will say which rule the drive broke.
