Rhode Island

RIDOT Hid Crash Data for Years Behind a Legal Excuse Most States Ignore. Now It’s Out: Nearly 35% of Crashes Have No Location, and RIDOT Sent Back $6.9 Million Meant to Fix It

Virtue Forge briefing · Nov. 17, 2026 · Based on Boston Globe reporting (Nov. 16 and Nov. 17, 2026), R.I. Gen. Laws § 38-2-17 (2026 Public Law ch. 221), a General Assembly press release (June 23, 2026), and 23 U.S.C. § 407

What happened

For years, the Rhode Island Department of Transportation refused to release its statewide database of traffic crashes. According to the General Assembly’s own press office, RIDOT cited “a federal law concerning its use in litigation.” When the Providence Streets Coalition appealed to the attorney general, the AG said RIDOT wasn’t legally required to release the records but urged it to consider the public interest. RIDOT kept them sealed.

In June the General Assembly made the data public by statute. The law, R.I. Gen. Laws § 38-2-17 (2026-S 2480 / H 7576, sponsored by Sen. Meghan Kallman and Rep. Rebecca Kislak), took effect June 19, 2026. It says records covered by the federal statute “shall nonetheless be deemed public records,” while barring uses the federal law prohibits.

The Boston Globe then got the data through a public records request. On Nov. 16 it reported what it found:

• Of the nearly 40,000 crashes reported in 2025, more than 12,000, “nearly 35 percent,” lacked information about the streets or intersections where they occurred. There was no location for 30% of injury crashes.
• Of 38 fatal crashes, 12 listed no specific location, including a single-car crash on I-95 that killed two 19-year-olds. Some records used highway exit numbers RIDOT changed years ago.
• In 2024 RIDOT received $6.9 million in federal money to improve its crash data collection, and gave it back. “While grateful for this funding, we ultimately decided that level of funding was not sufficient to meet required federal standards and the grant was returned,” RIDOT spokesperson Charles St. Martin told the Globe. Connecticut, the Globe reports, kept the $7.5 million it received.
• St. Martin put the missing locations on local police: “The data reports are filled out by officers in local police departments, and we do not contribute to them or edit them.”
• There is still no online portal. RIDOT says it intends to publish city- and town-level crash information “this fall.”

“It’s kind of a hopeless data set,” Providence Streets Coalition board member Jamie Pahigian told the Globe, describing data points “in the middle of water” and “nowhere near Rhode Island.”

On Nov. 17 the Globe reported that Gov.-elect Helena Foulkes wants Rhode Island to be “best in class” on public crash data. “It made no sense to me,” she said of the years of secrecy, and she called the returned grant “troubling.” Sen. Louis DiPalma told the Globe that his broader overhaul of the Access to Public Records Act “faced stiff opposition” from Gov. Dan McKee’s administration (the Globe’s words), and that the crash data piece passed only after advocates split it into its own bill.

Fact check: RIDOT’s legal excuse. The federal statute, 23 U.S.C. § 407, says highway-safety data compiled under certain federal programs “shall not be subject to discovery or admitted into evidence” in a federal or state court proceeding, or considered in a damages action. It limits how the data can be used in lawsuits. It does not bar a state from publishing it. That’s why, per the Globe, 42 states put crash data on public dashboards or maps and three more release the raw data. The AG’s office took a narrower point: Rhode Island’s public records law didn’t require release before 2026. That’s different from saying federal law forbade it. One more caveat: the 35% figure comes from the Globe’s own analysis, and RIDOT says the location gaps start in local police reports. Both things can be true. Connecticut, the Globe reports, automatically rejects any police crash report submitted without GPS coordinates.

Virtue Forge commentary

Transparency isn’t a favor government grants when it’s convenient. Taxpayers paid to collect this data, and it is about public roads. RIDOT spent years leaning on a reading of federal law that most of the country doesn’t share. It took a new statute to get the records out. And once they were out, roughly a third of the crashes couldn’t be put on a map.

Then there’s the $6.9 million. A free-market reader could reasonably like an agency turning down federal strings. But RIDOT didn’t say it was saving taxpayers money. It said the grant wasn’t enough to meet the standard, and it still hasn’t met the standard. Connecticut kept its grant and requires GPS on every report. That’s a management choice, not a funding problem. Blaming local police for the gaps doesn’t hold up either: RIDOT runs the statewide system and decides what it will accept.

Bias check: The Globe deserves credit for doing the records request and the analysis, which the agency should have done itself. Two things for readers to know. The coalition quoted most often endorsed Foulkes, as the Globe discloses. And the fix advocates want is better data, not new mandates on drivers. Accurate public data is a precondition for spending road money wisely, whatever you think about bike lanes.

One more lesson: the secrecy lasted as long as it did because the McKee administration fought a broader public records overhaul. Rhode Island’s public records law is weak, and an agency can sit on information until the legislature forces it out one category at a time. The next DOT director, chosen through the national search Foulkes has promised, should publish the crash data in a usable portal, require locations on every report, and report back on the federal money RIDOT gave up.

What to watch: whether RIDOT actually publishes the town-level data it promised “this fall,” whether the 2027 session revives the full public records overhaul, and whether RIDOT reapplies for crash data funding.
