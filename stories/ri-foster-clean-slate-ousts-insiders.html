Foster Voters Fire an Insider Council: Write-In ‘Clean Slate’ Ousts Four Incumbents After a Nepotism Fine and an Ethics Probe of the Town’s Public Works Boss

Virtue Forge briefing · Nov 28, 2026 · Based on The Providence Journal (Nov 24, 2026), The Local Insider (Nov 24, 2026), Rhode Island Current (Sept 1, 2026) and Rhode Island Ethics Commission Complaint No. 2024-12

What happened

In a Rhode Island election year short on upsets, the rural town of Foster produced one. Three write-in candidates and an independent unseated four of the five incumbents on the all-Republican Town Council. The Providence Journal and The Local Insider both reported on Nov. 24 that the result is now official.

The winners, per The Local Insider, are write-ins Linda Niles (independent), Susan Joyce (Democrat) and Nicole Andrescavage (Republican), plus independent Kathleen Swanson, who was on the ballot. Only council president Steven Plouffe held his seat. Incumbents Christie Stone, Catherine Bay, James Barrentine and Heidi Rogers lost. Heidi Rogers is the wife of Republican state Sen. Gordon Rogers, who is also Foster’s Public Works director. The Journal identifies him as Senate Minority Leader; Rhode Island Current called him the Senate minority whip in September.

The trio didn’t start campaigning until Oct. 13, the day before early voting opened. The Local Insider reports that they ran on “transparency and accessibility in local government,” against “wasting money on avoidable lawsuits,” and for “no secret deals or agendas,” and urged residents to vote for councilors “who aren’t just in it for themselves.”

The backdrop is a string of ethics problems around the Rogers household and Town Hall:

Nepotism fine. In December 2025 the Rhode Island Ethics Commission approved a settlement in which Heidi Rogers admitted two violations of the state nepotism rule and paid a $1,000 civil penalty. According to the commission’s order, on July 18, 2024 she took part in the council’s discussion and votes on whether to appoint an interim Public Works director “to replace her spouse, who was serving as the Director in a holdover capacity,” and whether to post the permanent job.

Ethics probe of the director. On Sept. 1, 2026, Rhode Island Current reported that the commission had voted unanimously to investigate a complaint against Gordon Rogers. The complaint accuses him of using town equipment, personnel and material in June to improve access to part of his own property, which he leases to a used-car dealer. Rogers then asked the council to pay for independent counsel of his choosing for the investigation. The Local Insider reports that the council was criticized for agreeing to have the town fund his legal defense.

Lawsuits. The Local Insider also notes that four town employees had sued the town and Gordon Rogers, alleging a hostile work environment.

Accuracy check: The investigation of Gordon Rogers is not a finding. Rogers told Rhode Island Current that the road is public, that he is responsible for maintaining it whoever leases the land beside it, and that a “working director” doesn’t need council permission for routine road work. “I welcome an objective review of the facts and am confident that I will be fully vindicated,” he said. The hostile-work-environment claims are allegations. Heidi Rogers’ $1,000 penalty was a negotiated settlement covering two votes on a single day. Rogers kept his Senate seat; he ran unopposed this year. We have left out precinct-level figures and rely only on the totals both outlets reported as official.

Virtue Forge commentary

Self-government in its rawest form looks like this. There was no party machine and no outside money, and nobody needed a spot on the printed ballot. Three neighbors started a month before Election Day, asked people to spell their names on the ballot, and fired a council that had grown comfortable. Write-in wins like this are close to unheard of in Rhode Island. The voters of Foster pulled one off anyway, and every incumbent in the state should be paying attention.

The underlying problem is an old one in small-town Rhode Island: too many hats on too few heads. One man runs the town’s road crews, sits in the state Senate, and is married to a member of the council that oversees his department. When the council voted on whether to replace him, his wife voted too, and the state’s ethics regulators called that nepotism. When regulators opened a probe into whether his crews improved a road that serves land he rents out, the council was asked to cover his lawyer. Even if every road job was routine, that arrangement fails the basic test of public trust. Taxpayers shouldn’t need a flowchart to work out who is overseeing whom.

Bias check: This was a Republican council in one of the state’s most Republican towns, and the challenger slate included a Democrat. Some will read the result as a partisan defeat. We don’t. The slate crossed party lines (one Republican, one Democrat, one independent), and the outgoing members point to real accomplishments: a finished ball field, a nearly complete police station, and new businesses on Route 6. The lesson isn’t about which party. One-party control at any level breeds insiders, and insiders start treating public office as a family business.

Accountability shouldn’t depend on a party label, and Rhode Island conservatives should welcome this result rather than mourn it. The state’s worst habits are the double-dipping, the family ties at Town Hall, and the taxpayer-funded defense of officials’ private problems, and those habits don’t become acceptable when they show up in a red town. The new council has a straightforward first assignment: post the Public Works job in the open, keep the town’s checkbook out of anyone’s personal legal fight, and get the stalled charter review moving again. Foster’s voters have already done the hard part.
