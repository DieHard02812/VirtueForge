Rhode Island

Cranston Voted 9-0 to Advance the Sale of Two Schools. Barrows Is Listed at $1.825 Million, Less Than Half the City’s $3.7 Million Assessment, With Proceeds Headed to a Rainy-Day Fund the City Says Was “Drastically Reduced” to Cover Deficits

Virtue Forge briefing · Nov. 17, 2026 · Based on the Cranston City Council’s Nov. 16, 2026 meeting video and agenda, GoLocalProv (Nov. 7, 2026), Cranston Online (Nov. 11, 2026), and the Oct. 7, 2026 city–school transfer agreement

What happened

On Monday night, Nov. 16, the Cranston City Council voted 9–0 to approve the Agreement for Transfer of Elementary Schools between the City and Cranston Public Schools, covering the Chester W. Barrows and Daniel D. Waterman school properties. Finance Committee Chair Andy Andujar presented the agreement and urged passage. After discussion, the clerk called the roll. All nine members — Bridget Graziano, Andy Andujar, Frank Ritz Jr., Michael Traficante, Emilia Vaziri, Richard Campopiano, Christopher Buonanno, Vice President Kristen Haroian, and President Daniel Wall — voted yes. The president announced that the motion carried. Official minutes have not been posted yet; the vote is taken from the city’s official meeting video, with the presentation at about 1:53:30 and the 9–0 roll call at about 1:59:39.

That vote approved the city–school transfer agreement. No sale has closed. Barrows, at 9 Beachmont Avenue, is on the market. Waterman is not listed yet.

Councilman Buonanno asked how the property could already be listed if the council had not yet approved the agreement, and, later, “What’s the listing price for each building?” Finance Director Robert Strom said the listing “has no bearing on the Council’s approval,” that “Right now, we just listed it, and it’s 180 day listing,” and that the city can’t sell it until the council approves. On the price, Strom said: “The asking price right now is $1,825,000.” He said that figure is for Barrows only. The city listed Barrows separately, he said, and would list Waterman if the Barrows sale succeeds. Strom also said the listing is for 180 days with a 5% commission, and that in the last week the listing broker reported 10 people had looked at the facility.

Vice President Haroian pointed out that “Warwick sold some schools recently, and they did not use a real estate agent.” She asked how Cranston had chosen its broker and said that in the future she thought the city should use a Cranston real estate agent.

GoLocalProv reported on Nov. 7 that principal Tom Sweeney of Sweeney Real Estate and Appraisal confirmed the Barrows listing price of $1.825 million, and that the city assesses the property at $3.7 million. Strom’s figure at the Nov. 16 meeting is the same asking price. $1,825,000 is an asking price, not a sale price.

Under the agreement, signed Oct. 7, 2026, the city pays the district $750,000 in two one-time payments: $500,000 after the first school is sold and $250,000 after the second, regardless of which is sold first. The city is to deposit the net proceeds of any later third-party sale into the city’s General Revenue Account. Cranston Online, reporting Nov. 11 on the Nov. 5 Finance Committee meeting, wrote that Strom said about $750,000 from the two buildings would go to the School Department — $500,000 from the Barrows sale and $250,000 from the Waterman sale — and that the city would use the remainder “to help rebuild its reserve fund.” The paper added that the city’s reserve fund, informally called the “rainy day fund,” “has been drastically reduced to offset recent annual budget deficits.”

Fact check: this is a transfer vote and an asking price, not a closed sale. The council approved the agreement that lets the city take the two school properties back from the district and market them. It did not approve a buyer or a sale price, and no sale has closed. $1,825,000 is the asking price Strom gave for Barrows only; Waterman is not listed yet. Official minutes are not on file as of this writing, so the 9–0 tally is from the official city video, not from a posted roll-call record. The $3.7 million comparison is the city assessment reported by GoLocalProv, not an independent appraisal quoted at the meeting.
The auditor’s maintenance-of-effort warning, and what the contract already says. Cranston Online reported that at the Nov. 5 Finance Committee meeting, David C. DiMaio, auditor and budget analyst for the City Council, “said it would be good practice to specify that any funds allotted to the school department from the sale of the two buildings are not part of the city’s annual support of local education. The distinction is important, DiMaio said, because under state law, a municipality cannot cut local funding for its schools from one year to the next.” The paper said the law is referred to as the “maintenance of effort” requirement, and that committee members agreed the school payments should be specified as one-time capital funding. The signed Oct. 7 agreement already contains protective language. It says each payment is a one-time, non-recurring payment, and: “The payments shall not be deemed to reduce, offset, substitute for, or otherwise affect any other obligation of the City with respect to school funding, operational support, or ‘maintenance of effort’ requirements under applicable Rhode Island or federal education law.” A following clause adds that the parties “affirm their mutual intent that the payments shall not be considered in any calculation that would reduce or increase the City’s statutory and regulatory ‘maintenance of effort’ obligations for education funding.”

Virtue Forge commentary

A 9–0 vote is not the same thing as a good deal. The council approved a transfer so two public school buildings can be sold. It did not close a sale, and it did not lock in $1.825 million. That number is the asking price for one building, Barrows, listed at less than half the $3.7 million the city itself assigns to the property. Waterman is still waiting its turn.

The process is the tell. The listing went up before the council voted. Strom said that didn’t matter, because a sale still needs council approval, and he described a 180-day listing with a 5% commission and 10 lookers in a week. Haroian’s question was the right one: Warwick recently sold schools without a realtor. If a public body is going to pay a commission to move a public building, it should be able to explain why, on the record, before the sign goes up.

Then there is where the money is supposed to go. The district is slated for $750,000 in one-time payments, $500,000 after the first sale and $250,000 after the second. The rest is supposed to refill a reserve that, as Cranston Online reported, “has been drastically reduced to offset recent annual budget deficits.” That is the city’s own description, not a slogan. Selling public buildings to refill that reserve is a choice taxpayers are entitled to see plainly. DiMaio was right to warn that the school payments cannot be treated as a way to cut the city’s annual support for schools. It is useful that the Oct. 7 agreement already says they shall not be used that way. Taxpayers should watch whether the city keeps that promise when the money actually moves.

Bias check: An asking price is not a sale price, and a city assessment is not automatically fair market value. The building may sell for more, for less, or not at all. This page does not claim the listing is a giveaway. It does say the council voted to advance the sale, the asking price is far below the assessed value, the listing preceded the vote, and the remainder is being pointed at a rainy-day fund the city’s coverage says was reduced to cover deficits.

What to watch: whether a Barrows sale comes back to the council, and at what price; when, or whether, Waterman is listed; and whether the $750,000 is treated as one-time capital and the net proceeds actually reach the reserve, without being used to lower the city’s maintenance-of-effort school funding.
