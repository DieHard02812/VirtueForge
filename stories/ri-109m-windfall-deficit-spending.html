Rhode Island Found $109 Million It Didn’t Expect and Passed a Millionaire’s Tax. It Still Faces a $240M-Plus Deficit. That’s a Spending Problem

Virtue Forge briefing · Nov. 14, 2026 · Based on Rhode Island Current (Nov. 11, 2026), the Providence Journal (Nov. 12, 2026) and the adopted November 2026 Revenue Estimating Conference worksheets

What happened

Rhode Island’s twice-a-year Revenue and Caseload Estimating Conference wrapped up on Tuesday, Nov. 10, and raised its forecast for general revenue in the current fiscal year (which ends June 30) to $6.154 billion. According to the adopted conference worksheets, that is $108,685,073 more than the estimate lawmakers used when they passed the fiscal 2027 budget in June. The Providence Journal notes that budget was a record-high $15.7 billion.

Where the extra money comes from, per the worksheets:

• Sales and use tax: up about $60.6 million, to $1.841 billion.
• Business corporation taxes: up about $49.5 million, to $432.8 million.
• Personal income tax: down about $24.9 million, to $2.148 billion. Rhode Island Current attributes that to lower-than-expected withholding.
• Lottery: down $300,000.

The windfall doesn’t solve the budget. The revenue estimate covers only the income side. When the fiscal 2027 budget passed in June, Rhode Island Current reports, the House Fiscal Office projected a $240 million deficit in fiscal 2028, growing to $540 million by fiscal 2031. And for fiscal 2028, the year the next budget covers, the conference projects general revenue of about $6.12 billion, about $34.9 million below the new figure for this year, before a hospital licensing fee is added.

Rhode Island Current also reports that the new surtax on income over $1 million begins in January, and that its expected $22 million boost to fiscal 2027 revenue has already been spent through the general fund.

The reactions:

• Gov.-elect Helena Buonanno Foulkes, who the Journal says will deliver her first budget to the General Assembly on Feb. 4: “I don’t think this will be an easy budget, even though revenue may come in higher than was planned.” She added, “We need to think about more than just one year,” and said the challenge would be “to be really disciplined about this.”
• House Speaker Christopher Blazejewski pointed to “punishing federal policy choices and the ongoing harm and uncertainty the federal government continues to inflict on Rhode Islanders.”
• Senate President Valarie Lawson also cited “the significant fiscal challenges posed by changes in federal policy.”
• Outgoing Gov. Dan McKee: “We are leaving Rhode Island in a better fiscal position than we found it.” He told the Journal the estimates “set the stage for an FY 28 budget that does not require raising taxes,” and said that “next week” he would share “more about our progress in closing the state’s structural budget deficit.”

Fact check: how big is the hole? The two outlets don’t agree on the number. Rhode Island Current cites the House Fiscal Office’s June projection of a $240 million fiscal 2028 deficit. The Providence Journal puts the House Fiscal Office estimate at $260 million “and growing.” Either way, the projected gap is more than twice the size of the $109 million windfall. Note too that the $109 million is a one-year bump in this year’s forecast. The conference’s own fiscal 2028 revenue figure is lower than this year’s. That dip comes mostly from about $231.5 million less in departmental receipts, while projected tax collections still grow.
Context: “a better fiscal position”? McKee can point to a bigger rainy-day fund and a better bond rating. But a state that ends a governor’s term with a record budget and a projected structural deficit of $240 million or more, still there after a nine-figure revenue surprise and a brand-new tax on top earners, isn’t in a strong position on spending. McKee says he will explain his progress on the structural deficit next week. We’ll look at the numbers when he does.

Virtue Forge commentary

Here is the pattern in one week of headlines. Revenue comes in $109 million above forecast, driven by Rhode Islanders spending money and businesses making it. A new millionaire’s tax hasn’t even started, and its first $22 million is already spent. And the state still faces a deficit of a quarter-billion dollars that grows to more than half a billion. When a government takes in more than it expected, adds a new tax, and still can’t close its books, the problem isn’t revenue.

The leaders who write the budget, all Democrats, offered a ready explanation: Washington. Federal changes to Medicaid and SNAP are real pressures, and Medicaid is about a third of the budget. But the House Fiscal Office’s deficit projection is built on a spending plan that Rhode Island’s own legislature passed in June. As our earlier look at RIPEC’s spending data showed, state and local spending per person has been growing faster than Rhode Islanders’ incomes. Blaming the federal government doesn’t change that.

Bias check: Rhode Island Current’s write-up framed the caution mostly around federal cuts under H.R. 1 and gave the last word on spending to new housing money. Neither outlet asked the obvious follow-up: which parts of a record $15.7 billion budget are growing faster than the economy that pays for them? That’s the question a disciplined budget has to answer.

The new governor says she wants to be “really disciplined.” Good. Discipline means treating a windfall as a chance to slow spending growth and return money to the people who earned it, not as seed money for a new round of programs. Watch Feb. 4: if the first budget closes the gap by spending less instead of taxing more, that will be real discipline.

What to watch: McKee’s promised structural-deficit update next week, the House Fiscal Office’s updated deficit figure, and the revised revenue forecast in May.
