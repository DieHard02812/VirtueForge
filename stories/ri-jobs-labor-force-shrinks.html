The 3.6% Mirage: Rhode Island’s Jobless Rate Fell Only Because 15,800 Residents Left the Labor Force—While Payrolls Shrank

Virtue Forge briefing · Based on the R.I. Department of Labor and Training’s September 2026 jobs release (Oct. 15, 2026), DLT’s seasonally adjusted labor-force series, Uprise RI (Oct. 15), Providence Business News (Oct. 15), and Providence Business First (Oct. 15)

What happened

On Thursday, Oct. 15, the Rhode Island Department of Labor and Training (DLT) reported that jobs at Rhode Island businesses fell by 1,600 in September, to 513,100. The state’s seasonally adjusted unemployment rate slipped to 3.6 percent, from 3.7 percent in August and 4.3 percent a year earlier. The U.S. rate was 4.2 percent.

The rest of the release is less flattering than that 3.6 percent. Compared with September 2025, according to DLT:

• Employed Rhode Island residents: down 11,000, to 556,300.
• Unemployed residents: down 4,700, to 20,800.
• Labor force: down 15,800, to 577,000.
• Labor-force participation: 62.0 percent, down from 63.9 percent a year earlier. The national rate is 61.8 percent.
• Total nonfarm jobs: down 700 (−0.1 percent), while U.S. jobs rose 0.3 percent. Private-sector jobs were up just 100 over the year.

DLT’s own seasonally adjusted series shows the labor force shrinking in every month of 2026, nine straight declines from 591,788 in December 2025 to 577,034 in September. It is now the smallest it has been since February 2023.

The September payroll losses came mostly from educational services (−1,200, which DLT said reflected “hiring expectations in September” that fell short), administrative and waste services (−600), and finance and insurance (−500). Construction and arts/entertainment/recreation each added 500. Manufacturing was flat. DLT also revised August down by 400 jobs, so August’s reported gain of 500 is now a gain of just 100. Over the past three months, the state has lost a net 100 jobs.

Fact check: The arithmetic holds up. Resident employment fell by about 11,000 over the year, so the lower unemployment rate cannot reflect more people working. It fell because the labor force shrank even faster (−15,800) than employment did. Using DLT’s unrounded figures, the labor force went from 592,799 to 577,034 (−15,765) and employment from 567,271 to 556,251 (−11,020). Two caveats about the coverage. (1) Providence Business First summarized it as the rate falling “mainly because people stopped looking for work.” DLT’s data show people leaving the labor force, but they don’t say why. Retirements in an aging state, people moving away, and discouraged job-seekers all count the same way, and the release doesn’t break them out. (2) Uprise RI wrote that the national unemployment rate “held at 4.2%” in September. DLT’s release says the U.S. rate was 4.2 percent, up one-tenth of a point from August. Uprise’s Rhode Island numbers match DLT.

Virtue Forge commentary

A falling jobless rate is supposed to mean more people are working. In Rhode Island this year it means fewer people are counted at all. Employed residents fell by about 11,000 in a year. Payrolls are slightly below last September, while the nation is up 0.3 percent. The private sector, the part of the economy that pays for everything else, added a rounding error of 100 jobs in twelve months. The 3.6 percent headline is accurate. It is also a mirage.

Now look at what state policy is stacking on top of that. The minimum wage is $16 an hour, more than double the federal $7.25 according to DLT’s own dashboard, and state law takes it to $17 on Jan. 1, 2027. Hasbro’s 587 Pawtucket jobs are headed to Boston, and they aren’t in these numbers yet. Voters are being asked to add $600 million in new bond debt to a state that already ranks first in debt per capita. A state that keeps raising the cost of hiring and borrowing against the future shouldn’t be surprised when the workforce quietly gets smaller. People don’t vote at the State House. They vote with a U-Haul, an early retirement, or a job across the line in Massachusetts.

Bias check: One month of state data is noisy, and DLT revised its prior-month estimate in each of its last four releases, per Uprise RI. State labor-force figures are model-based and get benchmarked every year, so the 15,800 could move. Rhode Island’s participation rate (62.0 percent) is still slightly above the national rate (61.8 percent), and demographics (an older population retiring) explain some of the drop no matter who governs. Hasbro’s exit is one company’s decision, not proof of a statewide trend. Our claim is narrower: nine straight monthly declines in the labor force, flat private payrolls, and falling resident employment are not what a thriving, competitive state looks like, and a low unemployment rate shouldn’t be allowed to hide that.

What to watch: the federal Bureau of Labor Statistics’ official September state figures (scheduled for Oct. 20, per Uprise RI); DLT’s October report on Nov. 19; whether Hasbro’s Pawtucket layoffs show up in the winter payroll counts; and whether any candidate on the Nov. 3 ballot treats a shrinking workforce as a problem to fix rather than a statistic to celebrate.
