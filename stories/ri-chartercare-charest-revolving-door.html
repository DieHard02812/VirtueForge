Revolving Door, Hospital Edition: McKee’s Former Health Secretary Will Run State-Backstopped CharterCARE—and Neronha Flags Ethics

Virtue Forge briefing · Based on GoLocalProv (Oct. 14–15, 2026), Providence Business News (Oct. 14, 2026), the Governor’s Office (Apr. 10, 2026), and Rhode Island Current / WPRI coverage of the $18 million state backstop (Feb. 2026)

What happened

On Wednesday, Oct. 14, 2026, the board of CharterCARE Health of Rhode Island, the nonprofit running Roger Williams Medical Center and Our Lady of Fatima Hospital, announced that CEO Jeffrey Liebman will retire Nov. 1. It named Richard Charest as his successor. Charest was Gov. Dan McKee’s Secretary of Health and Human Services (EOHHS) from 2023, and before that he was director of BHDDH. The governor’s office announced in April that he would retire from state service on July 3, 2026.

The state already has money riding on these hospitals. In February, McKee signed a law creating an $18 million backstop from the state’s supplemental rainy-day fund to secure the sale of Roger Williams and Fatima out of Prospect Medical’s bankruptcy. It can be tapped only if debt payments on roughly $87 million in bonds can’t be made. GoLocal reports that Charest “played a material role in negotiating the state’s finance deal with CharterCARE.” CharterCARE left bankruptcy six months ago. Its finances are under review by Attorney General Peter Neronha, and its board chair acknowledges “short-term cash flow pressures.”

Neronha’s statement on the leadership change: “We hope the Board has undertaken a diligent and thorough vetting process in selecting their next leader. We do note that given his current position at EOHHS, there may be some ethical considerations that would be most appropriate for the ethics commission to address.” A CharterCARE spokesperson said Charest will seek an advisory opinion from the Ethics Commission. Board chair Ben Mingle said the board found Charest “uniquely qualified over other candidates.” GoLocal also notes that Charest’s state financial disclosure lists a consulting business, 180 Degree Business Solution, LLC.

Fact check: There is a timing discrepancy. Neronha’s statement refers to Charest’s “current position at EOHHS,” but the governor’s April 10 release said he would retire from state service on July 3, 2026, and PBN calls him the ex-EOHHS secretary. We have not confirmed whether Charest kept any state role after July 3. Either way he left within the past few months. The claim that he “played a material role” in the state’s CharterCARE finance deal is GoLocal’s reporting, and we have not independently verified it. Whether Rhode Island’s revolving-door rules apply is an open question for the Ethics Commission, which has made no finding. No wrongdoing is alleged. The $18 million is a contingent reserve, not a cash grant, and the state’s exposure is capped at that amount.

Virtue Forge commentary

This is how Rhode Island works. The state puts taxpayer money behind a struggling hospital system, and a few months later the cabinet secretary who sat across the table becomes its CEO. Charest may well be qualified; turning around a bankrupt hospital (Landmark) is real experience. But public trust depends on officials who negotiate for taxpayers not later working for the other side of that deal. When the official who protected the public’s position goes to work for the counterparty, taxpayers are left asking whose interests were being served at the table.

Neronha deserves credit for saying the quiet part publicly instead of waving it through. The Ethics Commission should rule quickly and in public, and the board should release what vetting it actually did. If CharterCARE needs the state’s goodwill, the state’s backstop, and the state’s former health chief all at once, that is a hospital dependent on government, not a market turnaround. Virtue Forge has covered this before: Block’s warning about unverified turnaround math, and Neronha’s receivership watch.

Bias check: Safety-net hospitals serving hundreds of thousands of patients a year are worth saving, and experienced turnaround executives are scarce. Hiring a former state official isn’t corrupt in itself. The concern is the appearance of conflict and the absence, so far, of a ruling. That ruling, not this commentary, should decide the ethics question.

What to watch: Charest’s request for an Ethics Commission advisory opinion (the commission meets Oct. 20), any further AG action on CharterCARE’s revised plan, the nurses’ contract talks, and whether the $18 million reserve is ever tapped.
