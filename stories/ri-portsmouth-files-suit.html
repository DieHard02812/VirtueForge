Home Rule Goes to Court: Portsmouth Files Suit Against Rhode Island’s Housing Mandates

Virtue Forge briefing · Based on EastBayRI reporting (Jim McGaw, Sep 25–26, 2026)

What happened

On Thursday morning, Sep 24, 2026, the Town of Portsmouth filed a complaint for declaratory judgment and injunctive relief in Rhode Island Superior Court against the State of Rhode Island, Gov. Daniel McKee, and Attorney General Peter Neronha, EastBayRI’s Jim McGaw reported. The town argues that housing laws enacted in 2024, 2025, and 2026 unconstitutionally strip municipalities of control over local zoning, planning, and development, centering the challenge on Article XIII home-rule protections and contending that 13 provisions go too far.

Relief sought: declare that certain state requirements improperly interfere with municipal authority, and block enforcement of provisions found to violate home-rule protections. Filing alone does not change the authority of the Planning and Zoning boards to deny pending applications. Co-solicitor Giovanni Cicione told reporters, “The short answer is no,” and “Filing the complaint doesn’t get us there.”

At a Thursday evening press conference outside Town Hall (~40 people), co-solicitors Cicione and Joseph Larisa Jr. (lead counsel) spoke alongside Council VP David Gleason; council members present included Mary McDowell, David Reise, and Sharlene Patton. The council had voted 5-2 on Sep 14 to authorize up to $20,000; Payero and Blank opposed. Cicione said litigation could ultimately cost $100,000+. Hopkinton is the only other town committed money ($4,000). Twelve other municipalities supported a January resolution—Tiverton, Burrillville, Hopkinton, West Greenwich, Bristol, Smithfield, Lincoln, Middletown, Westerly, Charlestown, East Greenwich, and Exeter—but that support is not plaintiff status. Larisa: “No other town has seen the complaint until today.”

Cicione: “If these laws stand… Article 13 … doesn’t mean anything”; “We’ve let the lobster boil…”; “This suit is not about LMI.” Larisa called it “a constitutional case” and said, “Several of these laws do not allow the comprehensive plan to operate.” Context from coverage: the state housing push dates to 2021; the Executive Office of Housing reported nearly 3,800 units permitted statewide in 2025—the highest since the 1980s. Critics question cost and prospects; Payero called the suit “frivolous” and prefers a legislative path; Charles Levesque challenged the solicitors at the presser; a companion EastBayRI piece notes controversy over the Portsmouth Concerned Citizens’ role.

Fact check: This is a filed complaint, not a court ruling. As of EastBayRI’s Sep 25–26 coverage, no public case or docket number appears in the reporting—do not invent one. An injunction is not automatic; the town is still complying with the challenged laws, and Planning/Zoning boards retain their ordinary authority over pending applications until a court orders otherwise.

Virtue Forge commentary

Rhode Island needs more housing. Scarcity built by stacked state and local rules does not justify turning Smith Hill into a statewide land-use command center. Article XIII home rule exists so towns closest to roads, schools, sewers, wetlands, and tax bills retain meaningful say over zoning and comprehensive plans—not so Providence can rewrite those plans from a distance while municipalities absorb the infrastructure and fiscal costs.

Bias check: “Affordable housing” / housing-production framing from Smith Hill is the policy label, not proof every mandate expands supply or is constitutional. Filing a complaint does not prove every challenged provision is unlawful, and local boards can also function as a veto over new homes. Do not romanticize NIMBY obstruction; the test is whether rules secure property rights and permit more building under accountable government.

Portsmouth is right to put the boundary question before a judge rather than accept quiet centralization. Humble, competent government would show which mandates actually unlock supply, who pays for the consequences, and where home-rule lines still mean something—instead of boiling the lobster until Article XIII is empty words.
