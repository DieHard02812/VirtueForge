Rhode Island

The Speaker Pro Tem’s Kratom Junkets: Kratom-Linked Groups Paid $21,000 for Two Indonesia Trips He Didn’t Disclose While He Pushed Legalization. He Amended His Filings Only After the Globe Asked

Virtue Forge briefing · Nov. 22, 2026 · Based on Boston Globe reporting (Nov. 18, 2026) and Providence Journal reporting (Nov. 20, 2026)

What happened

State Rep. Brian Patrick Kennedy, a Westerly Democrat first elected to the House in 1988, is the longest-serving member of the Rhode Island House and its speaker pro tempore. He was the lead sponsor of the 2025 law that legalized kratom, overturning the state’s 2017 ban. According to the Boston Globe, while he pushed for legalization, Kennedy “took multiple all-expense paid trips alongside kratom lobbyists to Indonesia,” where he visited kratom farms and processing plants and met with officials involved in regulating exports to the United States.

Kennedy disclosed one trip: a $12,000, nine-day trip to Indonesia in 2022, which the Globe reports was paid for by the International Plant and Herbal Alliance, a nonprofit that supports kratom legalization. Two later trips did not appear on his annual ethics filings. The Globe reports the alliance paid nearly $15,000 for his July 2023 trip, according to the group’s president, Lora Romney, and the Center for Plant Science and Health, “a research nonprofit associated with the kratom lobby,” paid $6,000 for his August 2024 trip. Together, the two undisclosed trips cost $21,000. Rhode Island law requires public officials to disclose out-of-state travel worth more than $250 paid for by outside entities.

Over the same years, Kennedy disclosed “dozens of other trips paid for by outside groups” tied to his then-role as president of the National Conference of State Legislatures, the Globe reports. After the Globe contacted him, Kennedy said he amended his 2023 and 2024 disclosures on the night of Tuesday, Nov. 17. The Providence Journal reports that the amended 2024 filing describes the trip as “Meetings in Jakarta, Indonesia with Government officials, the scientific community, and members of the US consulate and American Indonesia Chamber of Commerce as part of astudy tour.” It also reports: “None of the disclosures include the word kratom.”

The timing tracks the bill. According to the Journal, Kennedy co-sponsored kratom bills in 2022 and 2023 that passed the House and died in the Senate. The 2023 trip was the first he did not disclose. In 2024 the bill passed the General Assembly, Gov. Dan McKee vetoed it, and Kennedy took his last Indonesia trip that August, after the veto. In 2025 Kennedy filed the bill as lead sponsor, it passed again, and McKee signed it. The Globe reports that Kennedy and American Kratom Association lobbyist Mac Haddow both said they met with McKee after the veto to present evidence on the plant’s safety.

On Wednesday, Nov. 18, End Kratom Addiction filed a complaint with the Rhode Island Ethics Commission. The Journal reports the complaint asks whether Kennedy violated rules requiring disclosure of travel benefits and accepted a gift from a business associate. It argues that Haddow’s seat on the NCSL board made him a business associate of Kennedy’s, which would have required Kennedy to recuse. “Legislative support for kratom, standing alone, is not alleged as an ethics violation,” said Hilary Tesluck, the group’s executive director. “The specific concerns are acceptance of potentially prohibited benefits, possible failure to disclose reportable benefits, and possible failure to recuse when a business associate presented evidence or arguments.” Ethics Commission executive director Jason Gramitt told the Globe the commission is expected to decide on Dec. 15 whether to investigate.

John Marion, executive director of Common Cause Rhode Island, told the Globe the travel should have been disclosed: “A normal Rhode Islander wouldn’t get a trip valued in the thousands of dollars to a foreign country out of the blue. Representative Kennedy only received this travel because he’s a public official, so it’s obviously designed to try to influence him.”

Kennedy’s side. Kennedy confirmed the trips to the Globe and said there was “nothing nefarious to be inferred” by the “inadvertent” omission from his ethics filings. He pointed out that neither group that paid for the travel is a lobbyist. Haddow confirmed that the American Kratom Association, which did lobby Rhode Island, organized and arranged the trips but did not pay for any of the travel. The Globe notes the association “is closely associated with” the Center for Plant Science and Health, and that the two share office space in Gainesville, Va. Romney called the alliance’s trips “fact-finding” missions: “This was a perfect opportunity for government leaders to learn so they can pass laws based on their educational findings.” Haddow said: “We follow all of the rules.” The Journal reports that Kennedy did not respond to its multiple calls.

Virtue Forge commentary

This is not a story about whether adults should be allowed to buy a plant. Legal commerce is legal commerce, and a free people can argue about kratom on the merits. Even the complainant says “legislative support for kratom, standing alone, is not alleged as an ethics violation.” This story is about honesty. The HouseThe House’s second-ranking officer spent yearsrsquo;s speaker pro tempore spent years pushing one industry’s top legislative goal. Meanwhile, groups tied to that industry paid for him to fly halfway around the world, twice, and the public never saw it on the forms the law requires him to file.

The disclosure rule is not obscure. The threshold is $250. Kennedy managed to report dozens of other sponsored trips. The two that went missing were the two tied to the bill he was carrying, worth $21,000 combined, and they appeared only after a reporter called. Even the amended 2024 entry describes a “study tour” without using the word kratom. “Inadvertent” may be the honest explanation. It is also the explanation any official would give, which is why the disclosure exists in the first place: so voters and colleagues can judge for themselves before the vote, not two years after the law takes effect.

The setup deserves attention too. A trade association organizes the trip, and an affiliated nonprofit that shares its office pays the bill. Common Cause’s Marion told the Globe it is common for lobbying groups to have separate nonprofits pay for trips to sidestep gift rules. In a one-party State House where the same people write the rules, enforce the rules on each other, and have held power for decades, disclosure is one of the few checks voters have left. When a speaker pro tempore skips it, that check fails. The Ethics Commission should take the complaint up on Dec. 15 and answer the questions in public.

Bias and accuracy check: Virtue Forge is skeptical of insider dealing in a General Assembly dominated by one party, and we have no objection to legal kratom sales. To be fair, Kennedy disclosed the 2022 trip, says the omissions were inadvertent, amended his filings, and notes that neither group that paid is a lobbyist. No finding of wrongdoing has been made, and the commission has not yet decided whether to investigate. One detail differs between the sources. The Globe says the International Plant and Herbal Alliance paid for the 2022 trip, while the Journal quotes Kennedy’s 2022 form as listing the Center for Plant Science and Health. We use the Globe’s per-trip figures, which come from the payers.

What to watch

• The Ethics Commission’s Dec. 15 meeting, and whether it opens an investigation into the disclosure, gift, and recusal questions.
• Whether any other lawmakers who voted on the kratom bills took similar sponsored travel.
• Whether the House takes up the law again. A House spokesman told the Journal that Speaker Christopher Blazejewski “believes the state statute needs reexamination, including serious consideration of a statewide ban on kratom.”
